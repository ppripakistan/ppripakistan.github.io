The Scale Imbalance: Why a 127-Million Citizen Province is an Outlier in Global Federalism

When a single subnational unit contains more than half of a federation’s entire population, conventional federal mechanics—from Senate equality to administrative responsiveness—face structural strain.

Author: PPRI Research Directorate Published: October 2026 Data Baseline: 2023 Digital Census Reading Time: 7 minutes
127.68M Punjab Population (Census 2023)
52.9% Share of National Population

1. The Structural Dilemma of Hegemonic Units

In comparative federal theory, federal stability relies on a delicate balance: no single constituent unit should be populous or economically dominant enough to outvote or dictate terms to the rest of the federation combined. Kenneth Wheare and Ronald Watts termed federations with a dominant unit "hegemonic federations"—systems where the center and the dominant province frequently conflate their interests, breeding peripheral grievance.

Pakistan presents an acute case of this structural condition. According to the 7th Population and Housing Census (2023), Punjab’s population reached 127,688,922 out of Pakistan’s total national population of 241,499,431. Punjab accounts for 52.87% of the country's citizens. If it were an independent sovereign state, Punjab would be the 11th most populous nation on earth, larger than Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom, or France.

2. Comparative Subnational Scale Across World Federations

How does Punjab compare to subnational entities in other democratic federations? With the single exception of Uttar Pradesh in India (241 million in a federation of 1.4 billion, comprising 17%), no subnational unit in the democratic world matches Punjab in either absolute scale or domestic majority share:

3. The Geographic and Administrative Penalty

The challenge of excessive scale is not merely political; it is fundamentally administrative. In a province of 127 million people governed from a single provincial secretariat in Lahore, geographic distance creates substantial friction for citizens seeking administrative redress, High Court relief, or institutional oversight.

For example, a citizen residing in Sadiqabad (Rahim Yar Khan district) is located approximately 640 kilometers from the provincial capital in Lahore—a 10-hour drive or costly rail journey. By contrast, that same citizen is only 520 km from Karachi and 180 km from Sukkur. Centralized administrative sanctioning of hospital equipment, school teacher transfers, and police disciplinary oversight in Lahore creates an unavoidable bottleneck for peripheral divisions such as Bahawalpur, Dera Ghazi Khan, and Multan.

The Counter-Argument: Institutional Transition Costs

Critics of subdividing Punjab point out that creating 2 to 4 new provinces from Punjab would incur substantial overhead costs: constructing new provincial assemblies, establishing High Court benches, duplicating departmental secretariats, and navigating civil service seniority splits. PPRI’s fiscal analysis indicates that these transition costs must be rigorously weighed against the productivity gains of decentralized administration.

4. Reconciling Scale: Two Policy Paths

The empirical evidence reveals that maintaining a 127-million citizen province under centralized provincial rule imposes measurable administrative costs on remote districts while perpetuating interprovincial mistrust at the federal level.

Policy analysts generally identify two distinct remedies:

  1. Territorial Reorganization: Subdividing the province along established divisional boundaries (such as South Punjab / Bahawalpur-Multan, Rawalpindi / Potohar, Central Punjab) to create units of 25–40 million citizens, conforming to standard international federal units.
  2. Deep Institutional Devolution (Article 140A): Empowering elected District Councils with constitutional fiscal shares (PFC awards) and decentralized administrative authorities, resolving citizen proximity issues without drawing new provincial borders.
