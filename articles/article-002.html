Beyond the 7th NFC: How Horizontal Fiscal Equalization Would Work Under a 16-Unit Federation

Every territorial reform scenario eventually confronts a public finance question: how would federal tax revenues be divided when four provincial stakeholders expand to sixteen?

Author: PPRI Research Directorate Published: October 2026 Series: Fiscal Federalism & Public Finance Reading Time: 8 minutes
57.5% Provincial Share of Divisible Pool
82.0% NFC Weight on Population
10.3% Poverty / Backwardness Weight
Art. 160(3A) Provincial Share Cannot Decrease

1. The Current Baseline: The 7th NFC Formula

Fiscal federalism in Pakistan is governed by Article 160 of the Constitution through the National Finance Commission (NFC). The 7th NFC Award (signed in 2009 and implemented from 2010 onward) established a landmark benchmark: the federal divisible pool (FDP) was divided vertically, with 57.5% allocated to the provinces and 42.5% retained by the federal government.

Furthermore, the 18th Constitutional Amendment inserted Article 160(3A), a constitutional ratchet clause stipulating that the share of the provinces in any subsequent NFC award shall not be less than the share given in the previous award.

Horizontally, the 7th NFC departed from sole reliance on population by adopting a multiple-indicator formula:

  • Population: 82.0% weight
  • Poverty & Backwardness: 10.3% weight
  • Revenue Generation / Collection: 5.0% weight
  • Inverse Population Density (IPD): 2.7% weight

2. The Challenge of Territorial Reorganization

Whenever the creation of new provinces is proposed—such as PPRI’s analytical 16-unit model or regional proposals for South Punjab, Hazara, or Karachi—the most contentious debates revolve around fiscal sustainability:

  1. Will newly established provinces possess sufficient Own-Source Revenue (OSR) to meet administrative overhead?
  2. How will horizontal shares of the 57.5% divisible pool be recalculated among 16 units?
  3. What happens to historical provincial liabilities, pension funds, and foreign project debt?
NFC Indicator Current 4-Province Dynamic Impact in 16-Unit Architecture
Population (82%) Punjab captures ~51.7% of total provincial pool due to single large population base. Disaggregates into 4 or 5 regional pools (e.g. South Punjab ~14.5%, Central Punjab ~21%, Potohar ~8%).
Poverty (10.3%) Balochistan and KP receive modest equalization adjustments. Significantly boosts historically marginalized divisions (e.g. D.G. Khan, Kalat, Tharparkar) that currently have their poverty obscured within wealthy provincial aggregates.
Revenue (5.0%) Sindh captures the largest share due to corporate tax headquartering in Karachi. Creates acute revenue concentration in urban city-provinces (Karachi, Lahore) unless revenue generation is measured at point-of-consumption rather than collection point.
Density (2.7%) Compensates Balochistan for immense geographic landmass. Allocated specifically to vast spatial units (e.g. Makran, Rakhshan, Zhob) rather than a single provincial treasury.

3. Simulating Equalization: The Urban-Rural Divergence

A critical finding of PPRI’s fiscal modeling is the "headquarters collection distortion." Under current arrangements, provincial taxes (such as Sales Tax on Services, stamp duties, and motor vehicle taxes) are disproportionately collected in major metropolitan centers like Karachi and Lahore, where national banks, telecoms, and multinational corporate headquarters are registered.

If Karachi or Lahore were delineated as separate administrative units within a multi-province framework, their initial per capita own-source revenue would appear overwhelmingly high, while surrounding agricultural or peripheral units (e.g. Mirpurkhas, Bahawalpur) would appear fiscally deficient.

The Equalization Mechanism: Formula Modernization

To prevent fiscal collapse in peripheral units, an updated 16-unit NFC award would require:
• Raising the Poverty and Deprivation weight from 10.3% to at least 18%.
• Calculating the Revenue indicator on provincial tax effort (OSR collected relative to regional GDP potential) rather than gross collection at ports or bank headquarters.
• Establishing a mandatory Federal Transition Equalization Grant (FTEG) for 10 years to finance new secretariat infrastructure without diverting development funds.

4. The Overlooked Key: Provincial Finance Commissions (PFCs)

Perhaps the most profound insight from comparative fiscal federalism is that territorial restructuring without sub-provincial devolution simply relocates centralized spending from one capital to another.

Under Article 140A, provinces are mandated to establish Provincial Finance Commissions (PFCs) to transfer untied resources to elected District Councils and Municipalities. In practice, all four provincial governments have routinely bypassed or underfunded PFC awards, retaining discretionary fiscal power at the provincial cabinet level.

A serious fiscal reform agenda must treat NFC reform and constitutional PFC guarantees as two halves of the same financial coin: ensuring resources flow not just from Islamabad to provincial capitals, but directly to district schools, clinics, and municipal drainage.
