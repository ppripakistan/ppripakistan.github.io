Before Local Government Faded, What Did Pakistanis Think About It?

Historical household surveys from the height of Pakistan’s 2001 devolution experiment provide rare empirical evidence of what citizens experienced when local institutions were fully functional points of contact.

Author: PPRI Research Faculty
By PPRI Public Opinion Research Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 10 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-016

Most debates over local government in Pakistan are conducted by a familiar circle of constitutional lawyers, provincial ministers, senior civil servants, and think-tank analysts. What is remarkably rare in policy archives is a rigorous, nationwide record of what ordinary citizens actually thought and did while an empowered local-government system was actively operating in their communities.

The 2004/05 National Social Audit of Governance and Delivery of Public Services, conducted across all four provinces during the operational height of the 2001 Devolution Plan, provides an indispensable empirical baseline. It asked households open-ended questions about whom they turned to when community crises emerged, how they evaluated their union councils, and how accessible state officials truly were.

The Most Revealing Metric: The Point of State Contact

The most striking empirical finding in the 2004/05 National Social Audit was not an abstract satisfaction rating. In an open-ended survey question—where respondents were not prompted with a curated multiple-choice list—citizens were asked: “Whom would you contact if something important needed to be done for your community?”

This is an extraordinary finding. In a country where national and provincial politicians routinely claim that constituents rely exclusively on them for local development, nearly half of all Pakistani households recognized the elected union representative as their real, practical entry point into the state machinery. Provincial and national parliamentarians combined accounted for barely one-sixth of the local councillor’s contact volume.

Accessible but Imperfect: The Nuance of Public Approval

Importantly, public engagement did not translate into unquestioning adoration. When asked to evaluate the new union council tier against the previous bureaucratic Commissionerate system:

  • 31% of respondents reported that the new union council system was distinctly better.
  • 25% believed it was about the same.
  • 18% judged it worse than the previous system.
  • 27% remained undecided or unsure.

These figures demonstrate a mature, grounded public reaction. Devolution was not experienced as an overnight miracle. In thousands of union councils, local nazims lacked the technical resources to fix deep-seated infrastructural decay. In fact, between 2002 and 2004, citizen satisfaction among those who actually contacted councillors dipped slightly from 54% to 48%, reflecting growing public frustration when accessible local leaders were blocked by provincial red tape.

Yet this highlights a vital institutional distinction: An elected local council can be accessible and flawed, which is vastly superior to a distant provincial secretariat that is virtually unreachable for a rural citizen.

Women as Tangible Points of Access

One of the structural breakthroughs documented in the 2004/05 audit was the transformative impact of the 33% mandatory gender quota. Prior to 2001, female presence in local governance was largely cosmetic. Under the 2001 Ordinance, tens of thousands of women entered local councils across Pakistan.

The Social Audit revealed that citizen satisfaction with local councillor interactions was statistically higher where the councillor was a woman. Women councillors provided female residents—who were frequently excluded from traditional patriarchal community dispute forums (panchayats or jirgas)—with a direct, socially acceptable avenue to access municipal services, register family disputes, and petition for female primary schooling facilities.

Citizen Community Boards: Co-Producing Development

The 2001 framework went beyond passive representation by instituting Citizen Community Boards (CCBs). Under the law, groups of non-elected local citizens could organize, contribute 20% of project costs in cash or voluntary labor, and legally claim an 80% matching grant from the local government development fund.

By 2006, over 7,200 CCBs had registered in Punjab alone, with Rs15 billion allocated for local projects ranging from village paved streets and water purification filtration to rural dispensary boundary walls. While procedural hurdles and audit complexities slowed early disbursements, CCBs demonstrated that when citizens are given statutory standing, community co-production flourishes.

The Core Policy Takeaway for PPRI Scenarios

The 2004/05 Social Audit delivers a decisive rebuke to the argument that ordinary citizens in Pakistan are indifferent to local government or prefer dealing solely with provincial legislators. When a visible, locally resident representative is endowed with formal legal responsibility, the public overwhelmingly uses that representative. Any future federal restructuring—whether through the creation of new provinces or municipal reforms—must place empowered, elected union councils at the center of institutional design.

Citizen Perceptions of Local Government in Pakistan (Gallup Pakistan & Public Survey Baseline)

Public opinion polling by Gallup Pakistan alongside historical field audits from the National Social Audit of Governance demonstrates consistent public demand for empowered municipal governance over distant centralized authorities. When surveyed regarding service delivery responsiveness, citizens consistently evaluate neighborhood Union Councils as far more accessible than provincial assembly legislators (MPAs).
