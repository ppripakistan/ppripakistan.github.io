Can Pakistan Grow Beyond Its Provincial Capitals? The Case for Stronger Secondary Cities

Pakistan’s urban crisis is not that its population is moving to cities, but that millions are funneling into only two or three provincial capitals. Developing polycentric intermediate cities is the country’s most urgent structural alternative.

By PPRI Urban Spatial Economics Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 12 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-019

For over half a century, Pakistan’s economic and urban narrative has been dominated by a handful of metropolitan names: Karachi, Lahore, Peshawar, and Quetta, with Islamabad functioning as an affluent federal administrative island. These primate cities absorb an overwhelming proportion of national infrastructure investments, top-tier medical facilities, universities, and commercial headquarters.

Yet beneath this primate urban canopy lies an immense, vibrant, and under-capitalized urban archipelago: Faisalabad, Rawalpindi, Gujranwala, Multan, Hyderabad, Sialkot, Sargodha, Bahawalpur, Sukkur, Larkana, Sahiwal, Jhang, Okara, Gujrat, and Rahim Yar Khan. These secondary cities are not mere miniature suburbs of provincial capitals. They are the true industrial, commercial, and agrarian engines of the country.

The Demographic Reality: Weight Outside the Capitals

According to the 7th Population and Housing Census (2023) conducted by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics (PBS), 22 urban localities have crossed the threshold of 500,000 residents: 15 in Punjab, 4 in Sindh, 2 in KP (Peshawar and Mardan), and 1 in Balochistan (Quetta). Ten Pakistani cities now possess metropolitan populations exceeding one million citizens.

The country’s twenty largest urban centers account for nearly 24% of Pakistan’s total population and more than 60% of its urban residents. The demographic scale already exists. The catastrophic policy failure is that the state continues to manage these secondary hubs as overgrown rural tehsils rather than autonomous economic locomotives.

Empirical Proof of Concept: The Punjab Intermediate Cities Program

Can intermediate cities raise their own governance standards if given the tools? The World Bank’s Punjab Intermediate Cities Improvement Investment Program (PICIIP) provides unambiguous empirical evidence:

World Bank PICIIP Results Review (June 2026)

Covering 16 intermediate secondary cities across Punjab—including Sialkot, Sahiwal, Bahawalpur, Sargodha, and Rahim Yar Khan—the program deliberately rejected one-off discretionary road schemes in favor of comprehensive institutional restructuring:

  • 3-Year Rolling Investment Plans: All 16 cities established multi-year capital investment programs backed by complete GIS-mapped asset inventories of water networks, drainage, and property boundaries.
  • Tripling Own-Source Municipal Revenue: Local municipal tax mobilization nearly tripled between FY2017/18 and FY2023/24, proving that local taxpayers will pay for transparent, visible services.
  • Institutional Performance Scores: Average municipal scorecard rankings surged to 90 out of 100, far exceeding the initial design target of 70.
  • 4.5 Million Beneficiaries: Over 4.5 million residents experienced measurable improvements in potable piped water delivery, wastewater interception, and engineered landfill management.

The Polycentric Economic Clustering Blueprint

Rather than compelling every ambitious graduate and industrial entrepreneur to migrate to Lahore or Karachi, a polycentric development model fosters specialized economic ecosystems based on existing regional competitive advantages:

Sialkot Industrial Cluster

Export Manufacturing

Global leader in surgical instruments, FIFA-grade sports equipment, and leather apparel; built Pakistan’s first private commercial airport and airline.

Faisalabad Textile Hub

Textile Value Chain

The Manchester of Pakistan; integrates spinning, weaving, garment processing, and specialized chemical dyes with direct motorway and freight connectivity.

Sargodha Citrus & Agro Zone

Agri-Processing & R&D

Global kinnow citrus capital; high-potential cluster for cold-chain logistics, fruit-processing plants, and specialized agronomic research institutes.

Gujranwala Engineering Arc

Light Engineering & Metals

Specialized fabrication of electric fans, aluminum cookware, sanitary fittings, and light agricultural machinery supplying domestic and Gulf markets.

Multan & Bahawalpur Hub

Commerce & Higher Education

Southern Punjab’s tertiary medical and university hub; regional commercial nexus for cotton ginning, pesticide distribution, and fertilizer logistics.

Sukkur Logistics Gateway

Corridor & Transit Hub

Strategic bridge connecting Upper Sindh, Balochistan, and Southern Punjab along the Indus Highway and M-5 motorway freight corridors.

Unitary Capital Primacy vs Polycentric Network

The contrast between Pakistan’s current primate city trajectory and a resilient polycentric urban network is structural:

Strategic Vector Single-Primate Capital Model (Current) Polycentric Secondary Cities Model (PPRI)
Infrastructure Spending Capital-city mega-projects (Rs31,000/capita in Lahore vs Rs7,000 rest of province). Formula-based capital grants across intermediate cities targeting water and sanitation.
Urban Land Markets Unchecked peripheral sprawl in Lahore/Karachi; agricultural land destruction. Compact urban containment in secondary cities with GIS-zoned master plans.
Climate & Environmental Risk Hazardous winter smog inversions; severe aquifer depletion in metropolitan capitals. Distributed environmental footprint; ecological preservation of natural water basins.
Administrative Distance Peripheral citizens must travel 6 to 10 hours for specialized medical and court services. Tertiary institutions accessible within a 90-minute commute across all sub-regions.

The Strategic Answer: Where Should the Next 20 Million Live?

Pakistan’s urban population will expand by over 40 million citizens before 2040. If national policymakers continue to channel this growth exclusively into Karachi, Lahore, and Islamabad, the result will be catastrophic infrastructure breakdown, acute water shortages, and toxic air crises. Strengthening secondary cities is not an optional aesthetic policy; it is Pakistan’s singular viable spatial hedge. Whether executed through provincial division or aggressive municipal empowerment, investing in intermediate cities is the defining urban challenge of the coming decade.
