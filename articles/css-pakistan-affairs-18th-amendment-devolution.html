Five constitutional references worth knowing

Core Constitutional Provisions for CSS Answers on the 18th Amendment and Devolution
Reference What it covers How to use it in an answer
18th Amendment and Article 142 Changes to the division of legislative authority, including removal of the Concurrent Legislative List. Explain why provinces gained greater responsibility in many policy areas. Check the relevant legislative list and constitutional provisions before making a claim about a particular function.
Article 25A The state’s obligation to provide free and compulsory education to children aged 5–16 as determined by law. Use it to frame school access, attendance, and implementation. Keep the age group visible when citing statistics.
Articles 153–154 The Council of Common Interests and its constitutional role in relevant federal–provincial matters. Explain why greater provincial autonomy still requires coordination on matters the Constitution assigns to the CCI.
Article 140A Provincial laws establishing local government and devolving political, administrative, and financial responsibility and authority to elected local representatives. Distinguish powers moving from the federation to provinces from powers moving from provinces to elected local governments.
Article 160 The National Finance Commission and arrangements for sharing eligible revenues between the federation and provinces. Connect responsibilities to resources. Distinguish the NFC from arrangements for provincial transfers to local governments.

Sources for the table are the consolidated Constitution of Pakistan (PDF) and the 18th Amendment Gazette (2010).

Model answer — How well have health and education performed after devolution?

Introduction. The 18th Amendment strengthened the role of provincial governments in planning and delivering health and education. Its performance should be judged by three things: whether provinces gained usable authority, whether outcomes improved, and whether improvements reached underserved districts and groups. On that test, the record shows gains in some indicators alongside substantial gaps.

Education. Provincial governments have room to tailor staffing, school infrastructure, and access programmes to local conditions. The challenge is that stronger provincial authority can coexist with large differences within provinces. PBS HIES 2024–25 put literacy among people aged 10 and above at 63% nationally, with 74% in urban areas and 55% in rural areas. That 19 percentage-point difference directs attention to the geography of opportunity. An assessment should also compare enrolment, attendance, completion, and learning outcomes. Literacy measures a broader accumulated outcome and changes over a longer period than a single education budget.

Health. Provincial responsibility gives governments room to organize primary care and outreach around local needs. National coordination remains valuable for issues such as disease surveillance and vaccine standards. In HIES 2024–25, PBS reported 73% record-based immunization coverage for children aged 12–23 months. That is one measure. An assessment should consider access to facilities, staffing, vaccine availability, and differences between regions. The indicator also depends on vaccine records, so a comparison needs the same age group and a consistent measurement method.

Why outcomes differ. Constitutional authority, administrative capacity, and the availability of funds affect different parts of delivery. A province can approve a school programme while failing to staff remote schools. A health department can procure vaccines while local outreach remains uneven. A before-and-after comparison should consider prior trends, measurement changes, funding, and local implementation before attributing the result to the amendment itself.

Way forward. Provinces should publish comparable district-level indicators and link them to budgets and staffing. Federal and provincial institutions should agree on common data definitions and coordinate on shared responsibilities. Within provinces, elected local bodies need clearly assigned functions, predictable funds, and accountable arrangements for agreed services. Each proposal should name which tier implements it and identify an outcome the public can check (Constitution, Articles 140A, 153–154 and 160).

Conclusion. Devolution gave provinces greater room to act. The next test is whether that authority produces better and more evenly distributed services. An assessment of the amendment should therefore connect three things throughout: the powers assigned, the resources available, and the outcomes residents experience.

Build your own answer

Use one page of notes per topic. Write a one-sentence thesis, then list two constitutional references, two comparable indicators, one counterargument, and three reforms with named institutions. In the exam, give each paragraph a claim, evidence, and an explanation that answers the command word.

Try these original practice questions

  1. Evaluate the argument that the 18th Amendment strengthened provincial autonomy while leaving local governments with weak practical authority.
  2. Compare the constitutional roles of the CCI and NFC. When might a dispute concern both coordination and finance?
  3. Assess whether creating smaller provinces would improve public services more effectively than empowering existing local governments.
  4. Explain how you would use literacy and immunization data to judge devolution. What can the figures establish, and what additional evidence would you need?

For Current Affairs, add one dated example to the constitutional framework. The new-provinces / 28th Amendment tracker works well because it forces a distinction between changing boundaries, reallocating money, and shifting control over services. For a question about provincial finance, pair this guide with PPRI’s Q1 FY2026–27 NFC transfers article, the PPRI Constitution hub, and PPRI CSS Fast Facts.

20-Mark Answer Checklist (2026 Question)

Sheet 1 (Pre-filled Example) — CSS Pakistan Affairs 20-Mark Answer Planner

Topic: Health & Education after 18th Amendment

1. Thesis (1 Sentence)

The 18th Amendment expanded provincial legislative and fiscal room in health and education, yielding measurable gains in some indicators alongside persistent urban–rural and local-delivery gaps.

2. Constitutional Mechanism

Art. 142 & Concurrent List removal; Art. 25A (free education age 5–16); Arts. 153–154 (CCI coordination); Art. 140A (elected local govt); Art. 160 (NFC transfers).

3. Evidence (Same Survey Series & Age Groups)

PBS HIES 2024–25: 63% literacy (age 10+), 74% urban vs. 55% rural (19 pp gap); 73% record-based immunization coverage (children aged 12–23 months).

4. Limitations & Counterargument

Authority alone does not staff remote schools or equalize outreach; before-and-after claims must account for prior trends, funding, and vaccine-record coverage.

5. Reforms (Named Tier + Checkable Outcome)

(a) Provinces publish district indicators linked to staffing/budgets; (b) CCI & federal/provincial bodies align definitions; (c) Provincial Assemblies fund Art. 140A local bodies predictably.

6. Conclusion

Devolution’s next test is evenly distributed services—connecting powers assigned, resources transferred, and measurable citizen outcomes.
