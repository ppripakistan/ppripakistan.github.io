Did Pakistan’s 2001 Devolution Plan Actually Improve Services? The Evidence Is More Complicated Than You Think

The 2001 Local Government Ordinance promised that shifting power from provincial secretariats to elected district nazims would revolutionize basic services. Household panel data and empirical audits reveal a nuanced, sector-specific reality.

Author: PPRI Research Faculty
By PPRI Service Delivery Evaluation Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 13 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-015

The 2001 Devolution Plan was heralded as the boldest structural reorganization in modern South Asian governance. By abolishing the historic administrative divide between rural and urban spaces, subordinating the powerful District Management Group (DMG) civil servants to elected District Nazims, and introducing formula-driven transfers, the reform promised to break the chokehold of provincial capitals over everyday services.

Two decades later, political partisans either celebrate 2001 as a golden age of municipal responsiveness or dismiss it as a military-era gambit that undermined provincial cohesion. Rigorous empirical research rejects both simplistic caricatures. The actual administrative and econometric record demonstrates that decentralisation succeeded in specific institutional dimensions while stalling in others due to severe, deliberate implementation bottlenecks.

The Empirical Service Delivery Scorecard

Instead of posing the sweeping ideological question, “Did devolution work?” policy analysts must ask the disciplined, sectoral question: “Worked at what?” An evidence-based disaggregation reveals starkly divergent performance across governance domains:

Primary Education Positive Impact

District-level econometric panel data across 34 Punjab districts between 2003 and 2015 confirmed a statistically significant positive relationship between subnational fiscal decentralization and primary school gross enrolment rates, driven by localized monitoring and prompt teacher absenteeism oversight.

Citizen Proximity Substantial Gain

The 2004/05 National Social Audit revealed an unprecedented realignment of public engagement: 46.8% of surveyed households named their Union Nazim or councillor as their primary interface for problem-solving, compared to just 7.3% for provincial or federal legislators.

Public Health Quality Modest / Disappointing

Ansari et al. (PMC/NCBI panel) observed that overall public satisfaction with state health units only edged up from 23% in 2002 to 27% in 2004, while the share of households regularly utilizing state dispensaries fell from 29% to 24%, driven by severe medicine stockouts and physician absenteeism.

Fiscal Autonomy Severe Bottleneck

Own-source revenue mobilization remained negligible. Districts remained more than 90% dependent on provincial formula transfers, which provincial finance secretariats routinely delayed, clawed back, or subjected to cumbersome procedural sanctions.

The Healthcare Conundrum: Why Devolution Didn’t Guarantee Quality

The findings from the comprehensive national healthcare panel (57,321 households in 2002 and 53,960 in 2004) published in PMC Health Services Research offer profound insights for institutional designers.

While satisfaction among people who actually utilized government health facilities rose from 63% to 67%, the overall proportion of citizens turning to the public health system declined. The qualitative focus groups attached to the survey identified unambiguous institutional drivers:

  • Pharmaceutical Deficits: Only 28.3% of patients in 2004 reported receiving their complete course of medicines from the facility dispensary, forcing low-income families into expensive out-of-pocket private purchases.
  • Absentee Medical Cadres: Doctors and specialists remained members of provincial health cadres rather than district employees. District nazims lacked statutory authority to dismiss or penalize chronic absentees, who maintained political patronage channels in provincial health ministries.
  • Financial Transfer Friction: District health officers consistently reported that sanctioned development funds for dispensary upgrades were released months late by provincial treasuries, lapsing at fiscal year-end before procurement could close.

This leads to a foundational governance insight: Decentralising the political title of oversight without decentralising career cadre control, hiring authority, and procurement autonomy does not fix public services. It simply decentralises citizen frustration.

The Education Panel: 34 Punjab Districts (2003–2015)

In contrast to health, primary education demonstrated measurable, quantifiable structural gains under decentralized administration. Research published in the Pakistan Journal of Poverty and Social Justice examined a 12-year longitudinal panel across 34 Punjab districts.

The econometric findings demonstrated that where district governments exercised greater fiscal discretion over school physical infrastructure (boundary walls, functioning toilets, clean drinking water filtration), primary-school gross enrolment ratios rose consistently. Why did education outperform healthcare?

Dimension Primary Education Delivery Basic Healthcare Delivery
Infrastructure Type Civil works (boundary walls, desks, toilets): easily executed by local contractors. Clinical equipment, cold-chain refrigeration, laboratory reagents: technically complex.
Staffing Mobility Locally recruited educators and contract teachers under district monitoring. Provincial medical officers seeking urban transfers to maintain private clinical practices.
Parental Visibility High daily community visibility via School Management Committees (SMCs). Episodic contact during acute illness; high information asymmetry between doctor and patient.
Outcome Measurement Headcount enrolment and examination pass rates easily tracked by district nazim. Maternal mortality and epidemiological containment require multi-tiered referral systems.

Devolution Without Decentralisation: The Implementation Bottleneck

A critical error in contemporary political discourse is measuring the 2001 reform as though it had reached its intended design destination. In reality, the reform operated in an environment of fierce institutional trench warfare between provincial bureaucracies and newly elected local bodies.

Provincial line departments fought vigorously to retain control over development schemes, posting authority, and procurement rules. District nazims frequently found that while they were held politically accountable by their constituents for bad roads or neglected clinics, the executive civil servants (DCOs and EDOs) continued to look toward the Chief Secretary in the provincial capital for their career promotions, confidential performance reports, and overseas training opportunities.

The Lesson for Modern Provincial Reorganisation

The 2001 devolution experience provides a vital empirical lesson for today’s new province debates. Simply moving a provincial capital closer to a region (e.g., creating a South Punjab province with a secretariat in Multan or Bahawalpur) does not automatically improve hospital care in Rajanpur or primary school standards in Layyah. If line departments in the new provincial capital repeat the same centralized administrative habits, the institutional failure will replicate itself. A serious reform must simultaneously pair horizontal re-demarcation of territories with vertical, statutory devolution of service delivery levers directly to municipal and tehsil bodies.
