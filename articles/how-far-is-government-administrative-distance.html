How Far Is Government? The Hidden Cost of Administrative Distance in Pakistan

“Government is too far away” is the most common political complaint in peripheral Pakistan. But distance is not merely kilometres on a map; it is travel time, financial friction, institutional hierarchy, and service deserts.

By PPRI Geospatial Policy Analytics Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 11 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-020

Across political rallies in South Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa’s Hazara belt, rural Balochistan, and Upper Sindh, a single phrase resonates with emotional power: “Hukoomat bohot door hai”—the government is too far away. Yet despite being the central emotional argument for provincial repartition, Pakistan has rarely quantified what “far away” actually means in empirical terms.

Is administrative distance 200 kilometres of highway? Is it an 8-hour bus ride across mountainous terrain? Is it the financial cost of two nights in a provincial capital hotel to obtain an arms license or pension clearance? Or is it the total absence of a pediatric tertiary ICU within a four-hour ambulance radius? To move the provincial debate from political slogans to scientific policy, PPRI introduces the concept of the Administrative Access Index (AAI).

The Six Dimensions of Administrative Distance

Administrative distance is multidimensional. Two citizens living precisely 250 kilometres from their respective provincial secretariats experience completely different realities of state accessibility:

1. Geographic Distance

Physical Euclidean mileage. While easy to calculate, it fails to capture topography, terrain friction, and seasonal impassability.

2. Time Isochrone Distance

Hours required to reach the decision-making office via public transit or motorways. A 100km mountain road can take 5 hours, while 100km on an M-tag motorway takes 50 minutes.

3. Financial Friction Distance

Direct economic burden of travel: transit fares, lost daily wages, accommodation, meals, and informal expediting fees for administrative intermediaries.

4. Information Asymmetry Distance

Citizen uncertainty regarding which secretariat department or desk holds legal jurisdiction, resulting in repeated futile bureaucratic visits.

5. Institutional Discretion Distance

Whether local field officers (ACs or Tehsildars) possess statutory authority to grant approvals, or whether documents must be physically routed to the provincial capital.

6. Political Access Distance

Whether an ordinary resident can petition their elected representative directly without relying on an hereditary feudal or clan intermediary.

Moving Beyond Maps: The Functional Devolution Test

The quantitative simulation reveals an essential policy insight: Moving the provincial capital shortens the travel time to the secretariat, but it only fixes citizen well-being if tertiary healthcare, universities, and district finances are decentralized at the exact same moment.

If a peasant farmer in Rojhan still has to travel three hours to Multan for dialysis because the local Tehsil Headquarters (THQ) hospital lacks a nephrologist and water filtration plant, the creation of a new province has failed its primary test. The ultimate goal of provincial reform is not simply redrawing boundary lines; it is eliminating administrative distance so that every citizen can access justice, healthcare, and accountable government within their own community.

The Scientific Frontier of Pakistan’s Devolution

By replacing rhetoric with geospatial analytics and travel isochrone indices, PPRI aims to transform Pakistan’s provincial debate. When a political faction demands a new province, the debate must no longer rest on ethnic identity alone. It must be subjected to quantitative scrutiny: How many citizen travel hours will this reform eliminate? How will it rebalance subnational expenditure? And will it truly dismantle the administrative barrier between the Pakistani citizen and the state?
