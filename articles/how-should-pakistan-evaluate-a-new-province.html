How Should Pakistan Evaluate a Proposal for a New Province?

The national debate on new provinces is frequently reduced to emotional slogans, identity assertions, or short-term partisan arithmetic. A better approach is to test every territorial proposal against the same constitutional, fiscal, administrative, and public-interest criteria.

By PPRI Research Faculty Published: October 2026 Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-010 ~11 min read

Pakistan does not suffer from a shortage of passionate arguments regarding new provinces. It suffers from an acute shortage of objective, standardized institutional tests.

One region argues that it is too geographically distant from the provincial capital. Another asserts that its commercial revenue subsidizes distant hinterlands. A third contends that its distinct linguistic identity is unconstitutionally marginalized. Conversely, opponents argue that creating new provinces will provoke ethnic friction, burden the federation with massive administrative payrolls, and distract from empowering local district governments.

All of these claims have merit in specific contexts. The fatal flaw in Pakistan’s public discourse is that these arguments are rarely evaluated through the exact same analytical process. To fill this vacuum, PPRI has formulated the 12-Point Empirical Evaluation Rubric—a rigorous, non-partisan standard that must be applied to any territorial reform proposal before it can be deemed governable.

The PPRI 12-Point Empirical Evaluation Rubric

Every serious legislative proposal—whether for South Punjab, Hazara, Karachi, or a broader 12-unit restructuring—must satisfy twelve objective benchmarks:

01

Constitutional Feasibility

Does the proposal articulate a credible legal pathway under Article 239(4)(4), securing the necessary two-thirds supermajority in both the federal Parliament and the concerned Provincial Assembly?

02

Democratic Public Consent

Is support verified through independent, region-specific demographic research and consultations, rather than political rally speeches or non-binding assembly declarations?

03

Administrative Access & Distance

Does the proposed boundary substantially reduce citizen travel times to the High Court, Civil Secretariat, and tertiary hospitals, without creating new inaccessible peripheries?

04

Institutional Capacity & Cadres

Does an operational plan exist for apportioning civil service cadres (PAS, PSP, provincial management), transferring court dockets, and staffing 40+ departmental directorates?

05

Fiscal Viability & Recurrent Space

Can the new unit generate sufficient own-source revenue and absorb existing debt and pension liabilities without permitting recurrent salaries to crowd out health and education budgets?

06

Equalization & NFC Realignment

How will the horizontal divisible pool formula (Article 160 (NFC)) adjust to compensate for backwardness and terrain costs without violating the Article 160(3A) ratcheting clause for remaining provinces?

07

Economic Geography & Markets

Does the boundary respect integrated economic basins, agro-industrial supply chains, and freight corridors, rather than severing commercial centers from their natural hinterlands?

08

Identity & Internal Minorities

While acknowledging regional linguistic aspirations, does the constitutional proposal guarantee statutory representation, judicial protection, and language rights for internal sub-minorities?

09

Local Government Devolution

Does the proposal include a binding Article 140A framework that constitutionally guarantees formula-based revenue sharing to its own districts, preventing the recreation of localized capital centralism?

10

Natural Resources & Water

Does the proposal include a clear intergovernmental compact for sharing river canal discharges under the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord, power royalties, and mineral extraction rights?

11

Transition Overhead & Budget

Has a 36-to-60 month transitional capital budget been formulated across low-cost, moderate, and full institutional scenarios, audited by an independent fiscal panel?

12

Measurable 5-Year Outcomes

Are explicit, measurable human development targets established in advance (child stunting, out-of-school rates, maternal health, municipal service access) to assess performance after 5 years?

Moving from emotion to evidence

The fundamental rule for Pakistan must be clear: no proposal should be endorsed simply because it sounds emotionally compelling or politically expedient, and no proposal should be dismissed simply because existing boundaries are familiar.

Whether analyzing South Punjab, Hazara, Karachi, or administrative divisional decentralisation, applying this 12-point rubric transforms a polarized constitutional standoff into a constructive policy science.

Frequently Asked Questions & Governance Insights

Verified policy answers regarding constitutional principles, empirical evidence, and administrative implications.

What is the PPRI 12-point empirical evaluation rubric for proposed provinces?

It evaluates proposals across demographic scale, fiscal viability, administrative proximity, recurrent cost overheads, water rights sharing, legislative balance, and local government empowerment.

Why should new province debates avoid purely emotional or political slogans?

Creating a province involves substantial recurrent capital costs (assemblies, high courts, secretariats). Without fiscal and administrative viability, a new province risks creating a localized bureaucratic elite without improving services.

What constitutional threshold is required to create a new province?

Under Article 239(4) of the 1973 Constitution, an amendment bill creating a new province must be passed by a two-thirds supermajority in both houses of Parliament and the affected provincial assembly.
