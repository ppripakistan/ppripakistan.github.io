Karachi and Urban Sindh: Representation, Revenue and Administrative Control

Karachi has become the most acute test of Pakistan’s debate over demographic scale, regional representation, and municipal autonomy. The question is not simply whether to create a province, but who exercises democratic and fiscal control over the metropolis.

Karachi is large enough to render almost every conventional governance framework in Pakistan inadequate.

According to the official 7th Population and Housing Census 2023, Karachi Division contains 20.38 million citizens (with approximately 18.87 million enumerated within the contiguous city boundaries). The division spans seven administrative districts: Karachi Central, Karachi East, Karachi South, Karachi West, Korangi, Malir, and Keamari.

Karachi is not merely a prominent provincial capital; it is a massive demographic, financial, and industrial empire situated inside Sindh. Its population exceeds that of over 140 sovereign states and represents more than 36% of Sindh’s total population of 55.7 million. That sheer demographic and commercial gravity explains why the debate over subnational restructuring reaches its fiercest intensity in urban Sindh.

The Urban vs. Rural Sindh Civil Service & Fiscal Quota

Under Sindh's constitutional quota system implemented since 1973 and renewed through provincial legislation, public sector employment, competitive civil service allocations (CCE/PCS), and professional admissions are bifurcated into a 60% Rural Sindh quota and a 40% Urban Sindh quota (restricted exclusively to Karachi, Hyderabad, and Sukkur). While Karachi accounts for over 36.6% of Sindh's official 55.7 million population and contributes over 68% of provincial tax revenue, its municipal budget authority remains fragmented across 18 overlapping federal, provincial, and military land-owning agencies.

Karachi’s core governance triad

The policy crisis in Karachi is routinely oversimplified as a binary dispute over provincial creation. In reality, it consists of three distinct structural dilemmas:

  1. The Metropolitan Scale Problem: How should a contiguous megacity of 20+ million people be administered when its municipal footprint crosses dozens of fragmented jurisdictions?
  2. The Revenue Attribution Debate: How should national corporate taxes, port import tariffs, and provincial service sales taxes collected in Karachi be fairly attributed and reinvested?
  3. The Local Devolution Deficit: How much executive, regulatory, and fiscal authority should be held by Karachi’s elected mayor and Town Municipal Corporations (TMCs) versus the provincial civil secretariat?

The fragmented governance mosaic: 18 overlapping agencies

The most crippling obstacle to public service delivery in Karachi is not only a lack of capital, but extraordinary administrative fragmentation. The metropolitan territory is partitioned across more than eighteen uncoordinated civilian, military, provincial, and federal entities:

Municipal Corporations

Karachi Metropolitan Corporation (KMC) alongside 25 elected Town Municipal Corporations (TMCs) and 246 Union Committees.

6 Military Cantonments

Cantonment Boards of Clifton, Karachi, Faisal, Malir, Korangi Creek, and Manora operating under federal Ministry of Defence jurisdiction.

Provincial Line Agencies

Sindh Building Control Authority (SBCA), Karachi Development Authority (KDA), Malir Development Authority (MDA), and Lyari Development Authority (LDA).

Water & Waste Corporations

Karachi Water & Sewerage Corporation (KWSC) and Sindh Solid Waste Management Board (SSWMB)—controlled by provincial board appointments.

Federal Maritime Ports

Karachi Port Trust (KPT) and Port Qasim Authority (PQA) exercising sovereign federal land and road estate control.

Federal Infrastructure Land

Pakistan Railways, Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), and National Highway Authority (NHA) controlling critical urban transit corridors.

When an elected mayor of Karachi lacks direct legal jurisdiction over the city’s water supply, building codes, solid waste management, and master planning across cantonment and port lands, municipal accountability collapses. Multiple agencies pass blame to one another while storm drains overflow and roads disintegrate.

The revenue debate: political claims vs empirical accounting

In July and August 2026, the political debate escalated when MQM-P leaders, including Federal Minister Mustafa Kamal, publicly asserted that Karachi generates over 90% of Sindh’s tax revenue and claimed the province had collected Rs 22 trillion over 18 years while allocating only Rs 680 billion to Karachi.

From an empirical public finance standpoint, such figures require rigorous context:

  • The Corporate Headquarters Distortion: Massive federal taxes (FBR income tax, corporate profit tax, customs duties) are formally booked in Karachi because multinational banks, oil refineries, and conglomerates maintain their principal corporate headquarters in Karachi South or Clifton. However, the economic transactions generating those corporate profits occur nationwide—from agricultural fields in Punjab to gas fields in Balochistan. Conflating corporate tax collection with local municipal tax generation is economically inaccurate.
  • Provincial Own-Source Taxation: In the realm of provincial revenue—specifically the Sindh Revenue Board (SRB) sales tax on services—Karachi indeed accounts for an estimated 65% to 75% of collections.
  • The Capital Expenditure Gap: While significant provincial funds are allocated to Karachi in annual budgets, treasury releases have frequently favored rural irrigation, canal lining, and rural road networks over municipal drainage and public transport in the metropolis.

The September 2026 Sindh Assembly confrontation

The renewed push for new provinces provoked a fierce constitutional clash in Hyderabad and Karachi, culminating on the floor of the Sindh Assembly:

Sindh Assembly Resolution 3309 (Passed 3 September 2026)

The Sindh Assembly unanimously passed Resolution 3309 forcefully rejecting any proposal to divide Sindh, alter its territorial limits, or carve out separate administrative provinces. The treasury benches (PPP) declared Sindh’s historic territorial integrity inviolable, while opposition legislators from MQM-P and PTI boycotted the final vote, arguing that demanding municipal devolution and administrative decentralization should not be equated with anti-Sindh sentiment.

This legislative clash underscores the profound legal barrier posed by Article 239(4). Because altering the borders of Sindh requires a two-thirds majority in the Sindh Assembly (112 votes out of 168), any initiative to create an independent “Karachi Province” or “Urban Sindh Province” without the consent of rural Sindh is constitutionally dead on arrival.

Three institutional pathways evaluated

Because unilateral territorial division is legally blocked by Article 239(4), policymakers must evaluate three concrete institutional pathways for governing Karachi:

Institutional Pathway Constitutional Mechanism Primary Strengths Risks & Feasibility Constraints
Pathway 1: Separate Administrative Province Article 239 constitutional amendment creating a 5th/6th province. Direct entitlement to NFC divisible pool; dedicated High Court, police service, and regional cabinet. Constitutionally blocked by Sindh Assembly 2/3 veto; risks severe ethnic polarization between urban and rural populations.
Pathway 2: Federally Administered Megacity Territory Constitutional transfer of Karachi Division to Federal Legislative List (similar to ICT). Direct federal funding, port-city infrastructure synergy, and national priority status. Violates 18th Amendment provincial autonomy; fiercely opposed by all major political parties across Sindh.
Pathway 3: Empowered Metropolitan Megacity Authority Reformed Sindh Local Government Act under Article 140A. Unifies KMC, SBCA, KDA, KWSC, and SSWMB under one directly elected Mayor; protected Provincial Finance Commission transfers. High political feasibility within existing borders; requires provincial assembly to surrender lucrative urban patronage.

The verdict: machinery over maps

Karachi’s plight demonstrates why simply redrawing maps does not automatically repair governance. If Karachi became an independent province tomorrow, but its administrative apparatus remained fragmented between military cantonments, municipal towns, and unaccountable civil servants, water tankers would still exploit residents and streets would still flood during monsoons.

The most urgent, realistic, and democratic solution for Karachi is Pathway 3: enacting genuine, constitutionally shielded local government devolution under Article 140A, unifying planning and municipal utilities under an empowered, elected Mayor, and instituting a formula-based Provincial Finance Commission that returns urban revenues to urban municipal infrastructure.

Frequently Asked Questions & Governance Insights

Verified answers to common public inquiries regarding administrative scale, constitutional procedure, and demographic benchmarks:

How many districts and towns are in Karachi in 2026?

Karachi Division is divided into 7 administrative districts (Karachi Central, Karachi East, Karachi South, Karachi West, Korangi, Malir, and Keamari) and 25 Town Municipal Corporations (TMCs), which encompass 246 elected Union Committees under the Sindh Local Government Act.

What is the official population of Karachi according to the 2023 Census?

According to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics 7th Census 2023, Karachi Division has a verified population of 20,382,881 citizens (20.38 million), making it Pakistan's largest urban locality and one of the largest megacities in the world.

What is the 60:40 urban vs rural quota in Sindh?

Introduced under provincial policy in 1973, the Sindh quota system reserves 60% of provincial government civil service jobs and professional university seats for residents of Rural Sindh, and 40% for Urban Sindh (defined strictly as the urban centers of Karachi, Hyderabad, and Sukkur), generating ongoing representation debates.

Why is Karachi's municipal administration fragmented?

Municipal governance in Karachi is divided across 18 separate federal, provincial, and military authorities—including the Karachi Metropolitan Corporation (KMC), 25 Town Municipal Corporations, 6 Cantonment Boards, Karachi Port Trust (KPT), Port Qasim Authority, Pakistan Railways, and the Defence Housing Authority (DHA)—preventing unified urban planning.
