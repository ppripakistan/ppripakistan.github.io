More Provinces or Stronger Local Governments? Pakistan's Devolution Debate

Pakistan does not necessarily face an all-or-nothing choice between territorial redivision and municipal local government. The real governance task is deciding which institutional level should control which decisions, taxes, and services.

By PPRI Research Faculty Published: October 2026 Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-006 ~11 min read

There are two statements frequently heard in Pakistani policy circles that sound completely contradictory, but are actually diagnosing the exact same structural failure:

“Pakistan must create smaller provinces to break the concentration of power.”
“Pakistan must empower elected local governments to bring power to the grassroots.”

Both statements are justified indictments of hyper-centralized governance. The disagreement is not about whether power is too concentrated in Pakistan—almost all analysts agree that it is. The sharp disagreement is about where the next layer of government should sit, and which institutional mechanism can realistically deliver accountable public administration.

The case for new provinces: political geography & representation

The case for creating new provinces rests primarily on political geography and the sheer scale of the existing federating units. When a single province contains 127.7 million people (Punjab)—accounting for 52.87% of the national population—or when a province spans 347,000 square kilometres of rugged terrain (Balochistan), administrative accessibility breaks down.

Citizens and regional leaders outside the provincial capital spend decades observing that their communities receive diluted political representation, minimal development attention, and weak bureaucratic responsiveness.

The intellectual strength of the new-province argument is not that smaller units are automatically efficient. Rather, it recognizes that a distinct geographic and cultural region may require its own political center—an elected assembly, a high court, and an autonomous share of the federal divisible pool under the National Finance Commission (NFC)—if the existing provincial secretariat repeatedly fails to address its unique structural grievances.

The case for local government: daily service delivery

The counterargument operates one tier closer to the ground. Proponents of local government observe that the vast majority of government services that determine a citizen's quality of life are not provincial responsibilities at all:

  • Municipal solid waste collection and street cleaning
  • Stormwater drainage and municipal sanitation
  • Local street lighting, tertiary paving, and traffic management
  • Urban zoning, building regulations, and spatial land-use control
  • Primary healthcare clinics and elementary school operational maintenance

These are fundamentally municipal and district functions. Under Article 140A of the Constitution, each province is already legally commanded to devolve political, administrative, and financial authority to elected local representatives.

Critics of territorial reorganization ask a pointed question: if the overarching objective is to bring government closer to the people, why invest hundreds of billions of rupees in creating new provincial parliaments, governor houses, and secretariats, instead of activating the municipal institutions that already exist on paper?

Head-to-head comparison: evaluating the two paths

To move beyond ideological posturing, PPRI has benchmarked both reform tracks across six objective institutional dimensions:

Dimension Path A: Creating More Provinces Path B: Empowering Local Governments Comparative Policy Assessment
1. Citizen Proximity Brings provincial capital closer (e.g. Multan vs Lahore), reducing travel distance to 100–150 km. Brings government to the municipal ward and union council (1–15 km from citizen doorsteps). Local government is vastly superior for immediate citizen proximity and daily municipal accessibility.
2. Transition Costs High capital costs: building new assemblies, high courts, civil secretariats, and minister fleets. Low-to-moderate costs: revitalizing existing municipal corporation and district council assets. Local government empowerment avoids creating massive new recurrent bureaucratic payrolls.
3. Constitutional Barrier Formidable: requires Article 239(4) double supermajority (2/3 NA, 2/3 Senate, 2/3 Provincial Assembly). Moderate: requires provincial assembly legislative amendments or a federal Article 140A enforcement act. Local government reforms face significantly fewer constitutional and territorial veto points.
4. Fiscal Viability Direct entitlement to NFC divisible pool; risks heavy recurrent payroll absorption. Dependent on Provincial Finance Commission (PFC) transfers and local property tax retention. Provinces have constitutional tax protection; local councils need stronger legal revenue guarantees.
5. Daily Service Delivery Indirect impact: provincial ministries still manage schools, police, and irrigation from a distance. Direct impact: elected mayors and councilors directly supervise trash, drainage, water, and streets. Municipal services improve far more reliably under empowered local councils than under new secretariats.
6. Political & Identity Needs Directly fulfills regional identity, cultural representation, and Senate legislative parity. Addresses administrative grievances but leaves macro-political and ethnic representation unchanged. New provinces are far more effective at resolving deep-seated regional identity grievances.

The hybrid devolution model: complementary, not contradictory

The most dangerous mistake in Pakistan's governance discourse is treating territorial reorganization and local government empowerment as mutually exclusive substitutes. They address fundamentally distinct layers of state architecture:

Tier 1: Provincial Tier

Primary Function: Regional Macro-Governance

  • Provincial legislation and statutory regulatory standards
  • Macro-economic planning, water rights & irrigation canals
  • Provincial police service cadre and High Court administration
  • Formula-based resource distribution to municipal tiers

Tier 2: Division Coordination

Primary Function: Administrative Synergy

  • Regional administrative commissioners coordinating across 3–4 districts
  • Inter-district highway networks and tertiary healthcare referrals
  • Disaster management, flood response, and environmental protection
  • Arbitration between conflicting municipal jurisdictions

Tier 3: Empowered Local Government

Primary Function: Direct Public Service Delivery

  • Urban municipal corporations and rural district councils
  • Autonomous collection and retention of urban immovable property tax
  • Operational oversight of primary education and basic health units
  • Citizen grievance redressal, spatial zoning, and waste management

A diagnostic reform decision tree

When should Pakistan pursue territorial subdivision under Article 239(4), and when should it focus on strengthening Article 140A local governments? PPRI offers a clear diagnostic logic tree for policymakers:

Diagnostic Question 1: Is the core grievance about daily municipal services?

If citizen dissatisfaction stems from uncollected garbage, contaminated water, broken local streets, or unstaffed primary schools, the remedy is Article 140A local government empowerment with protected municipal revenues. Creating a new provincial assembly will not fix a broken municipal sewer.

Diagnostic Question 2: Is the population or geographic scale paralyzing provincial administration?

If a federating unit exceeds 50–100 million people (such as Punjab's 127.7 million), or if geographic distances impose 10-to-15 hour journeys to reach the provincial high court or secretariat, administrative divisional devolution or territorial restructuring is justified to restore workable spans of control.

Diagnostic Question 3: Is there a distinct regional political identity demanding legislative parity?

If a region demonstrates sustained political consensus demanding distinct constitutional representation, regional cabinet autonomy, and Senate legislative parity, constitutional provincial status under Article 239 is the appropriate institutional instrument.

Diagnostic Rule: The Anti-Centralization Clause

Any newly created province must be legally bound by a reformed Article 140A framework that mandates automatic, formula-driven revenue transfers to its own districts and cities. A new province must never be permitted to become a miniature centralized monopoly.

The way forward for Pakistani federalism

Pakistan's federal debate cannot afford to remain trapped in emotional slogans. Lines on a map do not pave streets, and local councils cannot defend provincial constitutional rights in the Senate.

A mature 21st-century Pakistani federation requires both: sensibly sized provincial units capable of coherent regional planning, coupled with constitutionally fortified, financially autonomous local governments that serve citizens where they live.

Frequently Asked Questions & Governance Insights

Verified policy answers regarding constitutional principles, empirical evidence, and administrative implications.

Is creating new provinces or empowering local government more effective for service delivery?

Empirical evidence demonstrates that for daily citizen services (clean water, primary clinics, sanitation, local roads), empowered municipal local councils are far more cost-effective and responsive than creating high-overhead provincial secretariats.

Can new provinces and local government empowerment work together?

Yes. In mega-provinces like Punjab (127.7M people), restructuring into smaller provincial units can balance the federal Senate and NFC divisible pool, provided local governments are constitutionally protected within each new unit.

What happens if new provinces are created without local government?

Without empowered local councils, establishing new provincial capitals simply relocates centralized bureaucratic discretion to regional centers without delivering democratic power to ordinary citizens.
