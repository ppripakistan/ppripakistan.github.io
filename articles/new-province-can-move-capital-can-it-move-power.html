A New Province Can Move the Capital—But Can It Move the Power?

Creating a new subnational unit physically brings a provincial secretariat closer to a region. But without deep local government and dispersed institutions, a new capital simply reproduces the old hyper-centralized concentration of power.

Author: PPRI Research Faculty
By PPRI Spatial Governance Research Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 11 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-018

There is an instinctive, seductive simplicity to the political argument for creating new provinces in Pakistan. If Lahore is 500 kilometres away, establish a province in the south with its secretariat in Multan or Bahawalpur. If Peshawar is distant and culturally distinct from the Hazara belt, declare Abbottabad the provincial capital. If Karachi feels its revenue and municipal apparatus are suffocated by the wider Sindh administration, repartition the provincial boundary.

Moving the physical headquarters of government brings ministers, departmental secretaries, High Court benches, and development authorities closer on the map. But an uncomfortable second-order question is almost never asked in public rallies: What prevents the new provincial capital from behaving exactly like the old one?

The New Capital Centralization Paradox

The gravitational pull of state power is an established law of public administration. When a city is designated as a provincial capital, an immediate cascade of centralization begins:

The Centralization Feedback Loop in a New Province

  1. Secretariat Clustering: Government departments, directorates, and regulatory commissions concentrate in the designated capital city.
  2. Ancillary Professional Migration: Top legal chambers, consulting firms, major contractors, media bureaus, and non-governmental headquarters follow the executive apparatus.
  3. Social Infrastructure Distortion: Tertiary teaching hospitals, specialized research institutes, engineering colleges, and elite schools are disproportionately founded in the capital.
  4. Development Budget Concentration: Signal infrastructure (signal-free corridors, ring roads, metro bus conduits) absorbs the lion’s share of the new provincial ADP.
  5. Peripheral Resentment: Within 10 to 15 years, surrounding districts in the new province (e.g., Rajanpur, Layyah, or Kohistan) complain that their resources are being drained by the new capital city just as they once were by Lahore or Peshawar.

Creating a smaller province is not inherently synonymous with creating a decentralised province. If the new province maintains the same rigid provincial line departments, refuses to devolve budgets to district nazims, and demands that an ordinary citizen travel to the new capital for administrative clearances, the country has merely moved the geographic location of executive bottlenecks.

Capital Selection as a Science, Not Political Patronage

When politicians debate candidate capitals for proposed provinces—such as Multan vs Bahawalpur in South Punjab, or Abbottabad vs Haripur in Hazara—the competition is almost always waged through clan rivalries, real estate speculation, and legislative lobbying. PPRI proposes that capital selection must be treated as a rigorous, data-driven spatial optimization problem evaluated across ten concrete criteria:

1. Connectivity & Isochrones

Average travel time from the candidate city to the farthest peripheral district boundary via existing road and rail networks.

2. Water & Hydrological Security

Aquifer replenishment rates, canal water entitlements, and municipal groundwater table sustainability for the next 50 years.

3. Climate & Hazard Resilience

Exposure to seismic fault lines, Indus riverine flood corridors, heatwave vulnerability, and atmospheric inversion smog index.

4. Public Land Availability

Existing state-owned crown land reserves available for secretariat complexes without triggering predatory private agricultural acquisition.

5. Institutional Foundation

Existing capacity in tertiary healthcare beds, higher education faculties, and established High Court circuit infrastructure.

6. Spatial Balance Impact

Whether designating the city fosters polycentric growth or accelerates the economic cannibalization of neighboring regional towns.

The Dispersed Institutional Architecture: A Polycentric Blueprint

To prevent a new capital from swallowing a new province, the institutional design of new subnational units must mandate statutory institutional dispersion from day one. Rather than copying the unitary 19th-century colonial model where all state organs are crammed within one secretariat enclosure, key public institutions should be distributed across secondary cities:

Proposed Organ of State Traditional Unitary Model PPRI Dispersed Polycentric Model Institutional Rationale
Executive Secretariat Single Provincial Capital Designated Administrative Capital Houses Chief Minister and core policy coordination ministries.
Provincial High Court Capital City Principal Seat Established Historical Legal Hub Locates principal bench in existing legal center (e.g. Bahawalpur).
Agricultural & Water Research Provincial Capital Directorate Agrarian Heartland Secondary City Ties research institutes directly to farming basin (e.g. Rahim Yar Khan).
Public Service Academy Capital Suburb Intermediate Mountain / Foothill City Stimulates educational economy in secondary ecological zone.

The Litmus Test for Constitutional Scenarios

Whenever a bill or constitutional resolution for a new province is introduced in Pakistan’s Senate or National Assembly, citizens and researchers must apply a five-part litmus test: Did the reform move the boundary? Did it move political seats? Did it transfer independent fiscal revenue? Did it shorten hospital travel times? And most crucially: Did it transfer authority downward into elected district governments, or did it merely install a new chief minister in a closer palace? If power does not move downward to the third tier, changing the map is a cosmetic illusion.
