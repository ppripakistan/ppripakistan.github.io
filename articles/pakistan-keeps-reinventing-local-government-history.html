Pakistan Has Tried Local Government Again and Again: Why Does the System Keep Disappearing?

From the 1959 Basic Democracies to the 2001 Devolution Plan and post-18th Amendment statutory revisions, Pakistan has spent seven decades oscillating between bold grassroots decentralisation and abrupt centralist rollbacks.

By PPRI Institutional Governance Research Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 12 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-014

There is a curious and recurring rhythm in Pakistan’s political history. Local government never truly dies, but it never truly settles. A new regime arrives in power, denounces the centralized paralysis of the previous era, passes an ambitious local-body statute, and promises to bring the state to the citizen’s doorstep. For a brief few years, municipal halls buzz with activity, district nazims command bureaucratic respect, and resources flow to union councils. Then, inexorably, the framework is watered down, starved of cash, suspended, or legislatively repealed.

Years later, the state inevitably finds itself confronting the exact same administrative paralysis and begins the cycle all over again. Why has no local government settlement achieved the permanent constitutional and political durability enjoyed by the federal and provincial tiers?

A 70-Year Comparative Evolution: The Five Waves of Devolution

To understand why local government remains fragile, one must trace how the institutional purpose of the third tier has shifted across five distinct political epochs in modern Pakistani history:

1959 – 1969

1. The Basic Democracies Order (Ayub Khan Era)

Pakistan’s first formalized nationwide local-body architecture created a hierarchical four-tier system: Union Councils, Thana/Tehsil Councils, District Councils, and Divisional Councils. Over 120,000 elected Basic Democrats were tasked with local community development and municipal oversight. However, the system possessed a dual political architecture: councillors served as the direct electoral college for the presidential office and national parliament, bypassing direct universal adult franchise and inextricably linking local governance with military-regime legitimacy.

1972 – 1977

2. The People’s Local Government Ordinance (Zulfikar Ali Bhutto Era)

Following the collapse of the Ayub framework, the civilian government promulgated the People’s Local Government Ordinance (PLGO) of 1972. While promising progressive municipal democratization, local elections were repeatedly postponed nationwide as political authority centralized within parliamentary and party structures. The state pursued rural transformation through parallel technocratic channels such as the Integrated Rural Development Programme (IRDP), breaking institutional continuity.

1979 – 1988

3. The Non-Party Local Bodies Revival (Zia-ul-Haq Era)

The Local Government Ordinances of 1979 revived elected local councils across urban and rural divisions. Elections in 1979 and 1983 brought thousands of new political entrants into municipal administration. However, by strictly mandating non-party elections, the regime encouraged constituency competition along clan (biraderi), tribal, and personal patronage lines rather than institutional political platforms, fundamentally altering Pakistan’s grassroots political culture.

2001 – 2008

4. The Devolution Plan & District Government (Pervez Musharraf Era)

The 2001 Local Government Ordinance (LGO) represented the most radical administrative restructuring in Pakistan’s history. It abolished the historical rural-urban administrative divide, placed career civil servants (District Coordination Officers) under the executive command of elected District Nazims, established formula-based Provincial Finance Commissions (PFCs), and reserved 33% of council seats for women. The framework attempted to transfer roughly 40% of provincial revenue down to the district tier.

2010 – Present

5. The Post-18th Amendment Constitutional Era (Article 140A)

The 18th Constitutional Amendment inserted Article 140A, transforming local governance into a mandatory constitutional obligation for all four provinces. However, because provinces were granted exclusive legislative competence over local government design, provincial assemblies systematically enacted new laws that dismantled district nazim authority, restored bureaucratic primacy under Deputy Commissioners, starved councils of development grants, and frequently delayed elections through endless delimitation litigation.

The Paradox of Devolution in Pakistan

It has become commonplace in political commentary to argue that local government in Pakistan has simply "failed." The historical record refuses to validate so sweeping an indictment.

Different systems generated distinct, undeniable institutional strengths. The 1979 framework nurtured a generation of grassroots political leaders who later populated national politics. The 2001 Devolution Plan opened formal political space for hundreds of thousands of female councillors, created accessible dispute-resolution mechanisms at the union tier, and proved that formula-based fiscal decentralization could successfully deliver primary school improvements across 34 Punjab districts.

The persistent collapse of local government is not a failure of the local concept. It is the direct consequence of institutional instability:

“A municipal council requires multiple consecutive election cycles for citizens to learn who is responsible for what. A mayor requires five years of predictable revenue before a multi-year drainage or water master plan can be constructed. Bureaucrats need clear long-term tenure rules before they cease waiting for the next political regime to restore their executive dominance. Pakistan has repeatedly interrupted that learning process.”

The Civil-Military Cleavage: An Unintended Tragedy

A central tragedy of Pakistan’s local government history is that major decentralisation initiatives (1959, 1979, 2001) were championed by military governments seeking direct political legitimacy outside mainstream parliamentary parties, while civilian political parties, upon returning to office, viewed those local institutions as hostile instruments of regime consolidation.

Instead of reforming and institutionalizing the local tier under civilian oversight, democratic provincial cabinets routinely neutralized it, preferring to concentrate patronage funds among their parliamentary backbenchers. This created an unfortunate and historically inaccurate binary: the notion that local democracy is somehow antithetical to provincial parliamentary democracy.

Institutional Permanence: What Would it Take?

A durable local governance architecture cannot survive on goodwill alone. It requires constitutional fortification equivalent to the National Finance Commission (NFC) Award under Article 160: an explicit constitutional clause mandating that Provincial Finance Commissions must announce non-reducible awards, coupled with automatic ECP election scheduling upon the expiration of council tenures. Without structural constitutional insulation, Pakistan’s third tier will remain permanently vulnerable to the political whims of the provincial secretariats.
