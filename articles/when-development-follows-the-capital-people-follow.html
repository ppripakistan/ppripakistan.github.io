When Development Follows the Capital, People Follow: Is Pakistan Creating Its Own Urban Congestion Cycle?

A government office opens in the provincial capital. A university follows. Then a teaching hospital, expressways, and higher-paying jobs. Families notice the difference and migrate. Does concentrating opportunity create a self-reinforcing cycle of urban congestion?

By PPRI Research Faculty Published: October 2026 Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-011 ~11 min read

A government secretariat opens in the provincial capital. A public university follows. Then a tertiary teaching hospital, a modern ring road network, private academies, commercial banks, industrial estates, apartment complexes, and white-collar professional jobs.

Families in peripheral districts notice the stark difference in life prospects. Students move for higher education. Entrepreneurs move for market access. Rural laborers move for construction and service work. As demand explodes, urban land values surge, unplanned informal settlements sprawl outward, transport networks gridlock, and air quality collapses. The very city designed to radiate regional prosperity becomes an unaffordable, congested metropolis.

This dynamic is not accidental. While agglomeration economies naturally make large cities productive by bringing talent and capital together, Pakistan’s public investment pattern raises a fundamental constitutional question: what happens when public capital disproportionately concentrates in four provincial capitals at the expense of secondary cities and rural districts?

The demographic scale of the urban magnet

Pakistan’s official 7th Population and Housing Census 2023 demonstrates the extraordinary degree of spatial concentration. The twenty largest cities account for nearly 24% of the country’s entire population and more than 60% of its total urban population:

  • Karachi City: 20.38 million (Division: 20.38M) · 4.05% annual intercensal growth rate
  • Lahore: 13.00 million · 2.65% annual intercensal growth rate
  • Faisalabad: 3.69 million · Industrial textile hub
  • Rawalpindi: 3.36 million · Northern commercial hub
  • Gujranwala: 2.67 million · Manufacturing corridor
  • Multan: 2.22 million · Southern agrarian gateway
  • Peshawar: 1.91 million · Khyber Pakhtunkhwa administrative center
  • Quetta: 1.57 million · Balochistan administrative center

Karachi and Lahore alone account for almost 34% of Pakistan’s entire urban populace. As noted in the Asian Development Bank’s 2024 Pakistan National Urban Assessment, rapid, unmanaged urban concentration is generating severe municipal infrastructure deficits, chronic housing shortages, and deteriorating liveability across Pakistan’s metropolitan centers.

The Urban Congestion Feedback Loop

When public finance is concentrated at the center of political power, it triggers a self-reinforcing economic mechanism:

Stage 1: Concentrated Provincial Capital Spending

Provincial secretariats channel disproportionate development capital into the capital city for prestigious flyovers, expressways, tertiary hospitals, and elite universities.

Stage 2: Opportunity & Service Disparity Widens

Quality schooling, specialized healthcare, and formal private-sector employment become significantly more accessible in the capital than in secondary districts.

Stage 3: Accelerating Inward Migration

Households, students, and ambitious workers migrate from rural districts and small towns toward the capital seeking upward socio-economic mobility.

Stage 4: Urban Land Inflation & Infrastructure Strain

Surging demand drives real estate speculation, peripheral informal settlements (katchi abadis), severe traffic congestion, and groundwater depletion.

Stage 5: The Congestion Subsidy Trap

To relieve gridlock, the provincial government diverts even more public development funds into massive capital-city infrastructure projects, starving peripheral districts and restarting the cycle.

World Bank 2026 data: capital city vs peripheral spending

In its July 2026 assessment, Strengthening Fiscal Federalism to Drive Development, the World Bank quantified the per-person public spending disparities between provincial capitals and peripheral districts:

Province Capital City Per-Capita Outlay Rest of Province Per-Capita Outlay Disparity Ratio Primary Drivers & Institutional Caveats
Balochistan Rs 57,000 (Quetta) Rs 12,000 (Peripheral Districts) 4.75 × higher Provincial secretariats, tertiary hospitals, and security infrastructure clustered in Quetta.
Punjab Rs 31,000 (Lahore) Rs 7,000 (Peripheral Districts) 4.43 × higher Historical concentration in mass transit, ring roads, and specialized medical facilities.
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Rs 35,000 (Peshawar) Rs 10,000 (Peripheral Districts) 3.50 × higher Centralization of specialized tertiary healthcare and university campuses in the Peshawar valley.
Sindh Rs 25,000 (Karachi) Rs 14,000 (Peripheral Districts) 1.79 × higher High urban municipal operational cost balanced by substantial provincial irrigation investments in rural Sindh.

Editorial Note: Higher per-capita spending in provincial capitals is not automatically proof of administrative malevolence. Provincial capitals host specialized tertiary institutions, referral hospitals, and transit hubs that serve citizens arriving from every corner of the province. However, when capital spending permanently dwarfs basic school and primary healthcare funding in peripheral districts, it fuels regional grievances.

The Punjab Growth Strategy: acknowledging “Lahore-Centric” growth

The phenomenon was officially acknowledged in the Punjab Growth Strategy, which frankly described the province’s historical development trajectory as “Lahore-centric.”

In its baseline 2014–18 development outlay comparisons, the Strategy revealed that for every Rs 100 per capita allocated to Lahore, Multan received Rs 26, Bahawalnagar and Layyah received approximately Rs 10, and Pakpattan received roughly Rs 5. While these are historical benchmark figures, they illustrate the underlying logic: once a capital city accumulates advanced infrastructure, subsequent development projects become easier to justify economically, producing cumulative spatial inequality.

Would creating new provinces break the cycle?

Proponents of provincial restructuring argue that creating new provinces—such as in South Punjab or Hazara—would distribute capital investments by establishing new provincial administrative headquarters.

While a new provincial capital (e.g., Multan or Abbottabad) would inevitably attract public institutions, high courts, and commercial real estate development, it risks replicating the exact same centralization trap on a smaller territorial canvas. If Multan hoards public development resources at the expense of Rajanpur and Layyah, the geography of power shifts, but the underlying institutional failure remains intact.

The polycentric alternative: empowering secondary cities

Rather than relying solely on territorial redivision, Pakistan can adopt a polycentric urban development model that fosters secondary growth poles with predictable municipal revenues and planning autonomy.

A compelling practical model is demonstrated by the World Bank’s Punjab Cities Programme (PICIIP). Across 16 intermediate cities (including Sialkot, Sahiwal, Bahawalpur, and Rahim Yar Khan):

  • Over 4.5 million residents benefited from targeted municipal infrastructure upgrading.
  • Participating municipal corporations formulated 3-year rolling spatial plans backed by digital GIS asset registers.
  • Local own-source municipal tax collections nearly tripled between FY2018 and FY2024.

This empirical success proves that professionalized municipal management and predictable financial devolution below the provincial capital tier is achievable without constitutional deadlock.

Frequently Asked Questions & Governance Insights

Verified policy answers regarding constitutional principles, empirical evidence, and administrative implications.

What causes rapid migration toward provincial capitals in Pakistan?

Concentration of specialized healthcare, premier tertiary education, civil service jobs, and corporate headquarters in provincial capitals forces rural and secondary-city residents to migrate to the capital.

What are the municipal consequences of rapid urban centralization in Lahore and Karachi?

Severe groundwater depletion, air pollution (smog crises), transport gridlock, rapid proliferation of informal settlements, and skyrocketing municipal utility maintenance costs.

How does spatial decentralization mitigate urban congestion?

Distributing specialized hospitals, universities, and regional judicial courts across intermediate cities reduces the necessity for citizens to permanently relocate to provincial capitals.
