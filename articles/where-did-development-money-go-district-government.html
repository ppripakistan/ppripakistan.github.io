Subnational Fiscal Federalism & District Budgets

Where Did the Development Money Go When Pakistan Tried District Government?

The concept of devolution remains an abstract political slogan until it is converted into a budget line. How much public money reached districts under the 2001 system, how was it divided, and who held final execution control?

By PPRI Fiscal Federalism Research Desk
Published: October 3, 2026
Reading Time: 11 min read
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-017

The term “devolution” sounds enlightened in academic policy seminars. In the reality of public administration, however, decentralisation is defined by hard, unromantic fiscal mechanics: Did the money actually move into a local account? Who held the statutory power to sign the check? Did unspent balances lapse back to the provincial finance department on June 30? And could an elected district council cancel an unpopular provincial scheme to fund a clean water network instead?

Pakistan’s 2001 Devolution Plan attempted to institutionalize an ambitious answer to these questions. For the first time in the country’s history, public finance was systematically re-engineered to distribute resources through mathematical formulas rather than ministerial discretion.

39.80% Statutory interim share of the Provincial Consolidated Fund allocated to local bodies
67% / 33% Formula weighting: 67% population and 33% backwardness/index of deprivation

The District Fiscal Pipeline (2001–2008)

Under the 2001 Local Government Ordinance, subnational public finance operated through a formalized, multi-tiered pipeline designed to insulate development outlays from ministerial whim:

The Statutory Flow of Devolved Public Finance

1

Provincial Consolidated Fund (PCF) Retained Split

Under the interim Provincial Finance Commission (PFC) award, provincial net receipts were split into a Provincial Share (60.20%) and a Local Governments Retained Pool (39.80%).

2

Horizontal District Distribution Formula

The local pool was apportioned across districts using an objective two-factor formula: 67% weighted by Census population and 33% weighted by backwardness (infrastructure deficit, literacy, and poverty proxies).

3

Intra-District Vertical Devolution

A mandatory 25% share of the district’s development allocation was earmarked for direct downward transfer to Tehsil Municipal Administrations (TMAs) for municipal infrastructure.

4

Citizen Co-Production Reserve (CCBs)

A statutory minimum of local development capital was ring-fenced for registered Citizen Community Boards, providing 80% government matching grants against 20% citizen equity.

5

Non-Lapsing Development Balances

Unlike provincial ADPs—where unspent allocations traditionally lapsed back to the central treasury on midnight of June 30—local development funds were designed to roll over in district accounts, preventing wasteful fiscal year-end dumping.

Allocation Is Not Control: The Administrative Clawback

While the statutory architecture appeared robust on paper, an empirical examination of public accounts reveals a stark gap between nominal budget allocations and operational spending autonomy.

First, subnational development execution varied dramatically across provinces. As historical World Bank public expenditure reviews documented, while district development spending reached 15.7% of Punjab’s total development budget in FY2004/05, it accounted for barely 5.9% in NWFP (now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa), reflecting severe provincial reluctance to release funds.

Second, provincial bureaucracies quickly developed sophisticated administrative counter-measures to reassert control over the money:

  • Salary Encirclement: Over 85% of district budgets were swallowed by recurrent salary commitments for provincial cadres (teachers, medical technicians, revenue patwaris) whose pay scales and hiring were determined entirely by the provincial secretariat.
  • Procedural Sanction Chokepoints: Although district councils approved Annual Development Plans, technical sanctions for schemes exceeding modest financial thresholds required approval from the provincial Communication & Works (C&W) or Public Health Engineering departments.
  • The 2008 Budgetary Freeze: By 2008, as political power transitioned back to traditional provincial parties, provincial governments systematically stripped districts of discretionary capital budgets. A 2008 Dawn investigation in Rawalpindi revealed that the provincial government formally constrained district administrations strictly to recurrent salary maintenance, reclaiming all major development schemes back to Lahore.

The Six Accountability Questions: A Diagnostic Matrix

To evaluate whether any proposed subnational fiscal settlement—whether in an existing province or a newly created administrative state—delivers authentic decentralisation, PPRI applies a strict six-part diagnostic test:

1. Quantum of Transfer

What percentage of the consolidated fund moves to the local tier? (Is it above the 30% international benchmark?)

2. Formula Certainty

Is the transfer governed by a non-discretionary statutory formula, or can a minister unilaterally alter the share?

3. Approval Autonomy

Does the elected council possess full legal competence to sanction projects without central departmental no-objection certificates?

4. Budget Flexibility

Can the district reallocate surplus funds from an underperforming sector into urgent emergency local needs?

5. Fiscal Transparency

Are ward-level project allocations, contract award prices, and contractor identities accessible on open public dashboards?

6. Service-Level Impact

Did the capital investment produce measurable improvements in facility functionality, water purity, or student retention?

Fiscal Lessons for the New Province Agenda

A major justification for creating new provinces in Pakistan is that peripheral regions suffer from fiscal starvation at the hands of dominant provincial capitals. However, if a newly carved province simply institutes an identical centralist budget process in its own new secretariat—absorbing all NFC resources into provincial headquarters while offering crumbs to surrounding rural districts—the structural defect remains unresolved. Any serious constitutional amendment for new provinces must mandate an internal, statutory Provincial Finance Commission award that constitutionally locks in district shares before the new province even convenes its first assembly.
