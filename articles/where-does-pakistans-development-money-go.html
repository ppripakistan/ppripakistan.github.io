Where Does Pakistan's Development Money Go? Federal, Provincial and Local Spending Explained

Arguments about “unequal funds” often conflate completely different stages of public finance. Pakistan's real challenge becomes clear when we trace the public rupee from the federal pool down to provinces, districts, projects, and final service delivery.

Published: October 2026
Reading Time: 11 minutes
Lead Researcher: PPRI Fiscal Governance Unit
Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-005
57.5% Federal Divisible Pool transferred to provinces under the 7th NFC Award
>80% Provincial expenditure consumed by recurrent overhead (salaries, pensions, debt)

5 Tiers The public finance chain: Allocation → Release → Spend → Completion → Outcome

“Why didn't our area get the money?” is one of the oldest and most emotive questions in Pakistani politics. The trouble is that the answer is rarely contained in a single budget announcement or assembly speech.

Public finance in Pakistan moves through complex, institutional layers. There is the federal divisible pool governed by the National Finance Commission (NFC). There are provincial consolidated funds. There are recurrent operational expenditures and annual development programs (ADPs). There are district grants, treasury releases, revised estimates, and, eventually, whatever physical infrastructure or basic services citizens actually encounter on the ground.

By the time a political debate reaches a village school with collapsed boundary walls, a basic health unit without doctors, or an unpaved road, it becomes almost impossible for citizens to discern where the promised funding disappeared along the pipeline. This opacity is why the debate over new provinces is fundamentally a debate over fiscal federalism.

The Public Rupee Flow: following the money from Islamabad to the citizen

To understand why large fiscal transfers frequently fail to transform living standards in peripheral districts, we must trace the structural journey of public revenue across four distinct institutional tiers:

Tier 1: Federal Divisible Pool (7th NFC Award)

Federal-to-Provincial

The federal government transfers 57.5% of net divisible tax receipts to the four provinces. Provincial horizontal shares are determined by a four-part formula: Population (82%), Poverty/Backwardness (10.3%), Revenue Collection (5%), and Inverse Density (2.7%).

Punjab: 51.74% Population scale driver
Sindh: 24.55% Revenue collection weight
KP: 14.62% Post-merger NMD adjustments
Balochistan: 9.09% Inverse population density weight

Tier 2: Provincial Consolidated Fund & Recurrent Burden

Provincial Secretariat

Once federal transfers and provincial own-source taxes enter the provincial treasury, the vast majority is immediately locked into mandatory operational overhead.

Recurrent Overhead (>80%) Civil service salaries, pensions, policing, operational utilities, and debt servicing.
Development Space (<20%) Discretionary capital available for the Annual Development Programme (ADP).

Tier 3: The Annual Development Programme (ADP) Breakdown

Capital Allocation

The remaining capital budget is split between competing priorities, often prioritizing politically visible megaprojects over basic rural service delivery.

Provincial Megaprojects Highways, expressways, tertiary hospitals, mass transit in major urban centers.
Discretionary Constituency Schemes Political allocations channeled through MPAs and MNAs rather than institutional local councils.

Tier 4: District & Municipal Service Delivery

Citizen Interface

The final tier where services are delivered to citizens. Total expenditure undertaken by local governments has collapsed to under 5% of consolidated public spending nationwide.

Urban Municipalities Waste, drainage, streetlights, zoning (heavily dependent on provincial subsidies).
Rural District Councils Primary schools, rural dispensaries, farm-to-market roads (frequently paralyzed by unreleased funds).

Step two: provincial money is not the same as local money

This critical distinction is routinely obscured in public discourse. When a provincial government celebrates a record federal transfer under the NFC Award, citizens often assume their district will prosper proportionally.

Yet as documented by PIDE's 2025 study, provincial governments have systematically reduced the share of their revenues allocated to local tiers. In Sindh, transfers to local councils fell from 15.7% of provincial receipts in FY2012 to 5.8% in FY2026. In Punjab, the share fell from 28.0% to 19.1%. In Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, from 30.0% to 22.0%. In Balochistan, it dropped from an already meager 5.8% to an almost imperceptible 0.2%.

A province receiving unprecedented federal funding while simultaneously strangling its own municipal and district institutions does not represent decentralisation; it represents provincial fiscal concentration.

Step three: an allocation is not an expenditure

Budgetary announcements are political; actual service delivery is administrative. When a government announces a multi-billion rupee development package for an underdeveloped region, five distinct institutional hurdles must be crossed:

Stage Public Finance Mechanism Institutional Bottleneck / Risk Empirical Ground Reality
1. Allocation Annual budget speech and ADP white paper listing. Schemes announced without approved PC-I project documents or feasibility studies. Paper allocations designed to generate positive news headlines before elections.
2. Release Provincial Finance Department issues quarterly sanction letters. Treasury cash-flow rationing; releases withheld or delayed to fiscal Q4. Contractors face non-payment; construction work grinds to a halt mid-year.
3. Expenditure Treasury cheques cleared and contractor invoices debited. Lapse of unspent funds on June 30; bureaucratic procurement delays. Rushed end-of-year book adjustments that disguise uncompleted work.
4. Completion Contractor hands over physical structure to line department. Substandard materials, structural defects, absence of utility connections. Constructed hospital buildings standing empty without electricity or water.
5. Service Outcome Operational facility delivers tangible human development outcomes. SNE (Schedule of New Expenditure) unfunded: zero budget for doctors, teachers, medicines. “Ghost facilities” that consume capital without delivering public service.

South Punjab: a practical case study in budget mechanics

Consider South Punjab as an instructive case. In June 2026, the Punjab Finance Minister announced an allocation of Rs 556 billion for 387 development schemes in South Punjab for the 2026–27 fiscal year, alongside Rs 38 billion dedicated to Katcha riverine development.

On paper, Rs 556 billion appears to be a transformative regional capital injection. However, evaluating whether this allocation addresses historical regional grievances requires answering rigorous empirical questions:

  • Demographic proportionality: South Punjab accounts for approximately 31% of Punjab's population (40.4 million citizens) and over 45% of its geographic area. Does the allocation match its demographic share of the total provincial development portfolio?
  • Need-based weighting: Given that districts like Rajanpur, D.G. Khan, and Muzaffargarh exhibit multidimensional poverty rates double that of northern and central Punjab, does the allocation compensate for service-delivery deficits, or merely maintain baseline historical spending?
  • Actual treasury release: What percentage of the Rs 556 billion will actually be released across the four quarters, versus diverted to emergency flood relief or urban transport subsidies in Lahore?
  • Operational staffing (SNE): Have recurrent budget provisions been created to recruit doctors, technicians, and educators to run the newly constructed facilities?

The World Bank finding: historical inertia vs need

In its landmark 2026 review, Strengthening Fiscal Federalism to Drive Development, the World Bank uncovered a systemic flaw in Pakistan's public spending: district-level allocations continue to follow historical precedent rather than poverty rates or objective infrastructure deficits.

Historically endowed districts with influential political patronage networks reliably receive substantial capital allotments year after year. Conversely, peripheral, remote, or politically fractured districts remain trapped at the bottom of the funding distribution, regardless of their acute developmental distress.

If a funding formula does not explicitly measure district poverty, child stunting, maternal mortality, and geographic travel time, then increasing provincial budgets merely expands the volume of unequal spending.

The recurrent budget trap

Another structural constraint identified by the World Bank is that over 80% of provincial spending in FY2023 was recurrent. Civil service pay, pension liabilities, policing, operational maintenance, and interest payments consume four out of every five rupees entering the provincial exchequer.

This massive recurrent overhead leaves provincial governments with extremely constrained fiscal space for discretionary development investments. It also holds profound implications for new province proposals:

A newly created province would not simply receive a clean development slate. It would inherit a proportional share of existing civil service salary obligations and pension debts, while simultaneously incurring massive initial capital costs to establish a new provincial assembly, high court principal seat, civil secretariat, and departmental directorates. Without a major expansion in domestic revenue mobilization, administrative overhead could easily consume the very funds intended for schools and hospitals.

Four principles of transparent public spending

If Pakistan is to resolve its regional funding grievances—whether within existing provincial borders or through new administrative units—reform must adhere to four institutional principles:

1. Statutory PFC Awards

Provincial Finance Commissions must be made constitutionally mandatory, with formula-driven allocations that automatically flow to districts based on poverty, infrastructure gaps, and own-source collection effort.

2. District Budget Disaggregation

Every provincial budget white paper must explicitly publish district-by-district allocations, releases, and actual expenditures, eliminating opaque multi-district lump-sum development envelopes.

3. Digital Treasury Transparency

Implement public, real-time expenditure dashboards linked to provincial treasuries, allowing citizens, journalists, and researchers to track releases from approval to contractor disbursement.

4. Third-Party Outcome Auditing

Shift public audits from formal procedural compliance to independent physical verification: verifying whether constructed schools have enrolled students and whether water treatment plants deliver potable water.
