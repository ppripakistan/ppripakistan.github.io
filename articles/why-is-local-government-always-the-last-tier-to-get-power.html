Why Is Local Government Always the Last Tier to Get Power in Pakistan?

Article 140A guarantees political, administrative, and financial devolution to elected local councils. Yet subnational spending shares have halved since 2005 as provincial assemblies fiercely defend constituency patronage.

By PPRI Research Faculty Published: October 2026 Citation: PPRI-ART-2026-013 ~11 min read

Pakistan has conducted an intense national argument about decentralisation for decades. Islamabad is too far from the ordinary citizen, the argument insists, so political, legislative, and fiscal authority must devolve to the provincial capitals. Yet when that transfer occurs, an awkward second question immediately emerges: If provincial governments are created to bring the state closer to the people, why do so many daily decisions remain fiercely concentrated in provincial secretariats?

Pakistan’s Constitution provides an unambiguous answer on paper. Under Article 140A, introduced via the 18th Constitutional Amendment in 2010:

“Each Province shall, by law, establish a local government system and devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority to the elected representatives of the local governments.”

The constitutional text could hardly be more explicit. The fundamental failure is not the legal command; the failure is what systematically happens after the sentence is written into law.

<5% Local government share of total public spending in 2024 (down from ~10% in 2005)
5.8% Sindh provincial receipts transferred to local councils in FY26 (down from 15.7% in FY12)
19.1% Punjab receipts transferred to local bodies in FY26 (down from 28.0% in FY12)
10 Years Duration Punjab spent oscillating between dissolutions, litigation, and deferred polls

Local Government Is Where the State Becomes Tangible

Provincial assemblies debate statutory frameworks. Provincial departments publish policy directives. Provincial secretariats assemble multi-hundred-billion-rupee Annual Development Programmes (ADPs). But virtually everything a Pakistani citizen confronts on a daily basis is intensely and indisputably municipal:

  • The asphalt road outside the front gate and tertiary street repairs.
  • A burst sewage line or blocked stormwater drainage conduit.
  • Solid waste collection and landfill management.
  • Functional streetlights and neighborhood illumination.
  • Public park maintenance and neighborhood open space zoning.
  • Urban land-use enforcement, building codes, and setback compliance.
  • Potable water supply pipe networks and purification stations.
  • Primary civil registration: birth, death, marriage, and domicile records.
  • Fruit, vegetable, and livestock wholesale market regulation.

When the elected council physically closest to the problem possesses no independent budget line, no career municipal cadre, no statutory planning authority, and no execution powers, the constitutional promise of devolution degenerates into decorative administrative rhetoric.

The Public Finance Contraction: Starving the Third Tier

The statistical trajectory of subnational public expenditure in Pakistan demonstrates a steady centralization of state finances at the provincial tier. According to the World Bank’s 2026 assessment of Pakistani fiscal federalism, the local government share of total consolidated public spending declined from approximately 10% in 2005 to under 5% by 2024. Furthermore, district-level expenditures continue to mirror historic administrative precedent rather than dynamically targeting poverty incidence or municipal service gaps.

Independent research conducted by the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics (PIDE, 2025) documents a parallel erosion across provincial budgets:

Province FY2012 Local Share (% of Receipts) FY2026 Local Share (% of Receipts) Structural Trajectory
Sindh 15.7% 5.8% -63% reduction; heavy reliance on ad-hoc provincial discretionary grants
Punjab 28.0% 19.1% -32% drop; Provincial Finance Commission (PFC) awards routinely lapsed
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 30.0% 22.0% -27% decrease; village council development allocations repeatedly deferred
Balochistan 5.8% 0.2% Virtual elimination of rule-based municipal development financing

This financial strangulation is structurally crippling. A municipal corporation or union council cannot formulate a five-year capital improvement plan for storm drainage or wastewater treatment when its resource envelope is uncertain, unpredictable, and subject to quarterly bureaucratic sanctions from the provincial finance department.

The Political Economy of Resistance: The MPA vs Mayor Dilemma

A frequent political platitude suggests that provincial cabinets simply need to demonstrate enlightened goodwill and transfer more funds. This ignores the structural incentives governing subnational politics in Pakistan.

Under Pakistan’s first-past-the-post electoral ecosystem, Members of Provincial Assemblies (MPAs) and Members of National Assemblies (MNAs) do not win re-election based purely on legislative debates or statutory drafting. They win re-election by functioning as personal service brokers, securing asphalt schemes, transformers, tube-well connections, and school repairs for their specific voter blocs.

The Structural Patronage Conflict

As documented in the 2026 Governance Task Force findings reviewed by Dawn, the structural failure to institutionalize durable local bodies stems directly from competing institutional incentives between provincial legislators and municipal executives:

The Provincial Legislator (MPA)

  • Relies on constituency development funds (ADPs) to sustain patron-client networks.
  • Controls departmental postings, transfers, and contractor appointments in the district.
  • Views an autonomous city mayor as an existential threat to personal electoral survival.
  • Prefers unelected Deputy Commissioners who execute ministerial priorities without popular pushback.

The Elected Mayor / Council Chairman

  • Requires autonomous revenue and statutory control over municipal services.
  • Needs multi-year programmatic funding for comprehensive city-wide infrastructure.
  • Builds independent direct relationships with urban voters and civic associations.
  • Demands subordinate bureaucratic accountability to the elected municipal council.

These two models cannot comfortably co-exist within the same territorial constituency without friction. When provincial ministers and legislators hold exclusive legislative power in the provincial assembly, they routinely use that power to amend local government statutes, weaken municipal authorities, delay elections, or substitute autonomous municipal functions with provincial specialized companies (e.g., waste management companies, water and sanitation agencies, and development authorities) governed by board members appointed directly from the provincial capital.

The Punjab Experience: A Decade in Limbo

The procedural history of local government in Punjab offers an empirical case study of institutional disruption. Local bodies elections were held in phases in late 2015, but the elected councils were only allowed to take formal office in 2017 following protracted delays in indirect elections for mayors and district chairpersons. In 2019, those councils were dissolved prematurely before finishing their statutory tenure.

What followed was a dizzying sequence of legislative repeals, new statutory drafts, delimitation litigation, and executive postponements. In July 2026, the Election Commission of Pakistan (ECP) formally announced that it was logistically prepared to conduct elections across Punjab and 23 districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, explicitly reminding provincial administrations that local polls represent a non-negotiable constitutional mandate under Article 140A. Yet continuous statutory revisions repeatedly disrupted the timetable, resulting in the ECP proposing a December 14, 2026 polling schedule subject to final notification.

The institutional consequence is profound: An administrative institution cannot mature, train staff, optimize procurement, or establish citizen trust if its statutory architecture is wiped clean and rewritten every four years by incoming provincial administrations.

The Four Pillars of Durable Third-Tier Governance

Fixing the local government deficit requires more than passing another ordinance. A functional local tier requires four interdependent institutional pillars functioning simultaneously:

1. Protected Constitutional Tenure

Elections scheduled strictly within 90 days of council expiry by the ECP, with constitutional bars on arbitrary premature executive dissolutions.

2. Formula-Based PFC Transfers

Statutory Provincial Finance Commission awards that transfer non-discretionary, formula-based shares of the provincial consolidated fund directly to local bank accounts.

3. Unambiguous Functional Domain

Clear constitutional ring-fencing of primary healthcare, elementary schooling, municipal engineering, and urban zoning away from provincial line departments.

4. Subordinated Executive Cadre

A professionalized municipal service cadre reporting directly to elected mayors and council chairmen, rather than answering exclusively to provincial chief secretaries.

The Crucial Synthesis with the New Province Debate

The recurring demand for new provinces in Pakistan—whether in South Punjab, Hazara, or Urban Sindh—is frequently fueled by genuine grievances over municipal neglect and administrative distance from distant provincial secretariats. However, establishing a new provincial capital in Multan, Abbottabad, or Hyderabad solves only half the structural equation. If the newly formed province retains the same hyper-centralized executive apparatus and starves its own tehsils and union councils, power has merely moved to a closer secretariat without ever reaching the citizen. True devolution requires vertical depth just as much as horizontal division.

Frequently Asked Questions & Governance Insights

Verified policy answers regarding constitutional principles, empirical evidence, and administrative implications.

Why do provincial legislators resist empowering municipal local governments?

Provincial MPAs rely on discretionary development schemes (such as neighborhood paving, transformer installations, and local school repairs) as political patronage to secure re-election, which conflicts with autonomous local councils.

How does the 1973 Constitution treat the third tier of government?

Article 140A leaves the structure, tenure, and fiscal powers of local governments entirely to provincial assemblies, allowing provincial governments to alter or suspend local councils at will.

What international model could secure the financial autonomy of local councils?

Constitutionalizing a third-tier divisible pool share, similar to the 73rd and 74th Amendments in India or the 1988 Constitution in Brazil, where local governments receive a legally protected direct percentage of national taxes.
