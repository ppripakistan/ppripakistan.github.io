Why Is Pakistan Debating New Provinces Again?

Pakistan's provinces debate has returned with unusual force in 2026. The arguments are familiar—population growth, administrative distance, representation, regional inequality, and decentralisation—but the current debate is also asking a harder question: should power move to more provinces, to local governments, or to both?

There is something slightly strange about Pakistan's provinces debate. It can disappear for years and then return almost overnight, as if somebody has opened a cupboard and pulled out the same old map.

But the 2026 version is not quite the same debate.

Federal ministers have publicly supported smaller administrative units. Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal has talked about either creating 12–15 new provinces or, alternatively, establishing fully empowered governments in roughly 160 districts. Commerce Minister Jam Kamal Khan has argued that the 18th Amendment moved power from Islamabad to the provinces without a comparable transfer from provinces to districts. Federal Minister Sardar Mohammad Yousaf has described smaller units as a response to population growth and administrative pressure. At the same time, critics have argued that Pakistan should first make Article 140A local governments genuinely powerful rather than create another tier of political machinery.

This is why the question deserves more than another round of slogans.

Editorial Fact-Check Note: 2026 Political Claims vs. Census Baselines Public statements in late 2026 referencing population figures of 250–260 million reflect political projections. PPRI benchmarks all empirical comparisons against the official completed 2023 Census baseline of 241.50 million people. Claims regarding specific revenue allocations or public sentiment are treated as political arguments requiring evidentiary verification.

A country that became much larger without adding provinces

The official 2023 Population and Housing Census counted 241.50 million people in Pakistan. Punjab alone had 127.69 million, or 52.87% of the national population. Sindh had 55.70 million, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 40.86 million, and Balochistan 14.89 million. Islamabad Capital Territory had 2.36 million.

Those numbers matter because they change the scale of the problem. The administrative map inherited from an earlier period now has to carry a country that is vastly larger, more urban, and more complex.

Federal Minister Sardar Yousaf put the political argument in simple terms: as population increases, the challenge of governing it also increases, and smaller units could bring services and authority closer to citizens. That is the strongest version of the case for more provinces: not a love of maps, but a concern about the distance between an ordinary citizen and the institutions that make decisions about his or her life.

The distance problem is real, even if the solution is disputed

Think about what “distance from government” actually means. It is not only kilometres on a road. It can mean the distance between a district and a decision-maker; between a local problem and the department that controls the budget; between a school that needs repairs and the office that can approve them; or between a city government and a provincial department that controls municipal functions.

An August 2026 Dawn opinion piece described the issue in similar terms, arguing that administrative boundaries matter because they influence how quickly people receive education, healthcare, justice, infrastructure, and disaster relief. The same article, however, made an essential qualification: redrawing boundaries cannot by itself make government responsive.

A smaller province can move the provincial secretariat closer to people. It can create a new political centre. It can change which leaders are visible and accountable. But a smaller province can also reproduce the same centralisation problem on a smaller map. A powerful new regional capital can simply become the new place from which decisions are made for everyone else.

Path A: Territorial Reorganization (12–15 Units)

Mechanism: Subdividing provinces along administrative divisional boundaries under Article 239.

  • Shortens physical distance to provincial secretariats and courts.
  • Breaks the demographic majority of a single province in the federation.
  • Substantial transition costs: new assemblies, ministries, and High Court principal seats.
  • Requires complex constitutional supermajorities.

Path B: Article 140A District Devolution (164 Municipalities)

Mechanism: Statutory financial and administrative devolution directly to elected district councils.

  • Immediate proximity: water, sanitation, schools, and roads governed locally.
  • Avoids costly bureaucratic overheads and new legislative assemblies.
  • Requires no boundary revisions or ethnic realignments.
  • Historically vulnerable to provincial assembly recentralization and delayed elections.

The strongest argument in favour of new provinces is actually about representation

The argument that deserves more attention is not simply “large provinces are inefficient.” It is that large political units can concentrate representation and resources in a limited number of centres.

This is especially visible in the recurring demands from South Punjab, Hazara, and parts of urban Sindh. The language changes from place to place, but the complaint often has the same shape:

Decisions are being made somewhere else, by institutions that are too distant from the people affected by them.

The National Assembly's 2012 resolution in favour of a Janoobi Punjab province is revealing because it did not describe the issue only in ethnic terms. It said a new province was needed to address the “grievances” and secure the political, administrative, and economic interests of southern Punjab. Fourteen years later, that underlying institutional grievance remains active.

The money argument: who decides where it goes?

Regional inequality is often reduced to a sentence like “our area gets less money.” The real question is more complicated.

The World Bank's 2026 review of Pakistan's fiscal federalism found that district spending continues to follow historical momentum rather than poverty levels or service-delivery gaps. More than 80% of provincial spending in FY2023 was recurrent expenditure (salaries, pensions, operations), while local governments' share of total government spending fell from around 10% in 2005 to under 5% in 2024.

The problem is not just how much money is divided between Islamabad and the provinces under the NFC Award. It is that, once money reaches provincial capitals, the next distribution is not tied to need, outcomes, or local priorities. The question is not just who gets the money? It is who decides where it goes?

The constitutional barrier is not a footnote

Creating a new province is not an administrative order that can simply be announced on television. It requires a formidable constitutional consensus under Article 239.

Step 1: Federal Parliament

National Assembly & Senate

The constitutional amendment bill must be passed by a two-thirds supermajority of the total membership in both houses of Parliament.

Step 2: Provincial Consent

Affected Provincial Assembly

Under Article 239(4), if provincial limits are altered, the concerned provincial assembly must pass the bill by a two-thirds majority of its total membership.

Step 3: Enactment

Presidential Assent

Only after both federal and provincial two-thirds thresholds are satisfied can the bill be presented to the President for formal constitutional assent.

This constitutional barrier matters enormously in places where the existing province opposes territorial division. In September 2026, the Sindh Assembly unanimously passed a resolution rejecting proposals to divide Sindh or alter its territorial limits. Anyone discussing “new provinces” without confronting the Article 239(4) provincial consent requirement is discussing only half the reality.

What about public opinion?

This is where politicians and commentators often overstate their evidence. There is no recent national opinion poll that can credibly establish a current majority view on new provinces across Pakistan.

There is, however, an important historical Gallup Pakistan survey from January 2012. At that time, 29% nationally supported creating new provinces while 59% opposed it. But the provincial pattern was deeply divided: 51% in Sindh and 58% in Balochistan supported smaller units, compared with 22% in Punjab and 12% in KP. While those numbers are historical and must not be treated as today's public mood, they prove that opinion on provincial restructuring has never been uniform. The debate is regional as much as national.

The real debate is larger than the map

The most useful way to look at the 2026 debate is not as an ideological battle between “four provinces” and “many provinces.” It is as a question about the fundamental architecture of government.

Pakistan faces three distinct directions:

  1. Keep existing provinces but constitutionally enforce statutory local-government devolution under Article 140A.
  2. Create more provinces or administrative units and then devolve power downward to districts.
  3. Combine territorial restructuring with a serious fiscal equalisation and local-government settlement.

The strongest pro-province argument is that political authority should not remain concentrated in massive territorial units where citizens feel permanently peripheral. The strongest counter-argument is that drawing a new boundary does not automatically produce responsive institutions, revenue systems, or honest administration.

The sensible question is therefore narrower and far more demanding:

Under what conditions would a new province actually improve governance for the citizens living inside it?
