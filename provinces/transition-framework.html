Implementation

How would transition actually work?

Institutional reform is a process. A credible proposal needs a transition architecture before it can be evaluated seriously.

Stage 1 — Legal settlement

Identify the constitutional amendment, provincial legislation, institutional acts and other legal instruments required.

Stage 2 — Territorial and institutional design

Define the territory, administrative units, capital, departments, courts, police structure and relationships with local governments.

Stage 3 — Assets, liabilities and personnel

Inventory buildings, land, vehicles, records, contracts, public corporations, debts and employee positions. Establish allocation principles before transfers occur.

Stage 4 — Fiscal settlement

Set recurring revenue and expenditure arrangements and identify one-time establishment costs. Link assumptions to the NFC and other fiscal rules.

Stage 5 — Service continuity

Education, health, policing, courts, utilities and other public services should continue during the transition. A transition plan should identify responsible authorities and contingency arrangements.

Stage 6 — Institutional launch

Establish the new institutions, administrative systems, staffing structures and democratic processes required by law.

Stage 7 — Evaluation

Publish post-reform indicators so that the public can evaluate whether the stated objectives were achieved.
