RESEARCH FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

A plain-language guide to the questions that arise most often when discussing provincial reform in Pakistan.

1. How is a new province created in Pakistan under Article 239(4)?

Under Article 239(4) of the 1973 Constitution of Pakistan, any bill that alters the limits or boundaries of a province cannot be presented to the President for assent unless it has been passed by the Provincial Assembly of that affected province by the votes of not less than two-thirds of its total membership. Once approved by the provincial assembly, the bill must also pass both houses of the federal Parliament (National Assembly and Senate) by a two-thirds majority to amend Article 1 and Article 59 of the Constitution.

2. What is the population of Pakistan's 4 provinces according to the 2023 Census?

According to the official 7th Population and Housing Census 2023 conducted by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics (PBS), Pakistan's total population is 241,499,431. The province-wise breakdown is: Punjab: 127,688,922 (52.88%); Sindh: 55,696,147 (23.06%); Khyber Pakhtunkhwa: 40,856,097 (16.92%); Balochistan: 14,894,402 (6.17%); and Islamabad Capital Territory: 2,363,863 (0.98%).

3. Can Punjab be divided into South Punjab without a constitutional amendment?

No. Creating a legally autonomous province of South Punjab with its own elected Provincial Assembly, Chief Minister, Governor, High Court, and independent constitutional share under the National Finance Commission (NFC) Award strictly requires a constitutional amendment modifying Article 1, Article 59, and Article 106. However, an administrative secretariat (such as the South Punjab Additional Chief Secretary and Additional IGP secretariat in Multan and Bahawalpur) can be and has been established by executive provincial notification without altering constitutional boundaries.

4. What is the 7th NFC Award formula for sharing taxes in Pakistan?

Under the 7th National Finance Commission (NFC) Award signed in 2009, 57.5% of the federal divisible pool of taxes is transferred to the provinces. The horizontal distribution among provinces is determined by four multiple weighted criteria: Population (82.0%), Poverty/Backwardness (10.3%), Revenue Collection/Generation (5.0%), and Inverse Population Density (2.7%). As a result, Punjab receives 51.74%, Sindh receives 24.55%, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa receives 14.62% (plus 1% for the War on Terror), and Balochistan receives 9.09%.

5. What are the 25 towns and 7 districts of Karachi?

Karachi Division (18.87 million population in Census 2023) is organized into 7 administrative districts: Karachi Central, Karachi East, Karachi South, Karachi West, Korangi, Malir, and Keamari. Under the Sindh Local Government (Amendment) Act 2021, these 7 districts are subdivided into 25 Municipal Town Corporations, including Gulberg, Liaquatabad, North Nazimabad, Nazimabad, New Karachi, Gulshan-e-Iqbal, Jamshed, Safoora, Chanesar, Sohrab Goth, Saddar, Lyari, Orangi, Mominabad, Manghopir, Korangi, Landhi, Shah Faisal, Model Colony, Malir, Gadap, Ibrahim Hyderi, Keamari, Baldia, and Moriro Mirbahar.

6. What is Article 140A of the Constitution of Pakistan?

Article 140A of the Constitution of Pakistan mandates that each province shall, by law, establish a local government system and devolve political, administrative, and financial responsibility and authority to the elected representatives of the local governments. Clause (2) mandates that elections to local governments shall be conducted by the Election Commission of Pakistan (ECP).

7. Does Pakistan need 13 provinces or empowered local governments?

Policy analysis from PPRI demonstrates that administrative reorganization into smaller provinces (such as the 13-unit PPRI-01 model) and local government empowerment under Article 140A are complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Smaller provinces reduce bureaucratic distance to provincial secretariats and High Courts, while empowered District and Tehsil Councils ensure direct citizen control over primary municipal services such as water, sanitation, local roads, and secondary schools.

8. What is the difference between South Punjab, Bahawalpur, and Saraiki Suba?

While frequently conflated, they represent three distinct geographic and historical proposals: (1) South Punjab Province typically comprises the 3 divisions of Multan, Bahawalpur, and DG Khan (11 districts, ~40.4M population); (2) Bahawalpur Province is a restoration demand based on the historical Princely State of Bahawalpur that merged into One Unit in 1955 (comprising 3 districts: Bahawalpur, Bahawalnagar, Rahim Yar Khan, ~13.0M population); (3) Saraiki Suba is a linguistic-cultural demand that includes not only southern Punjab divisions but also contiguous Saraiki-speaking districts in KP (such as Dera Ismail Khan and Tank).

9. Why did the Hazara province movement start in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa?

The Hazara province movement gained mass political momentum in April 2010 following the passage of the 18th Constitutional Amendment, which officially renamed the North-West Frontier Province (NWFP) to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa without incorporating the ethno-linguistic identity of the Hindko-speaking Hazara division. The demand is driven by historical identity, linguistic distinction, geographic separation from Peshawar, and feelings of fiscal marginalization across Abbottabad, Haripur, Mansehra, Battagram, Torghar, and Kohistan.

10. What is the estimated administrative cost of creating a new province in Pakistan?

PPRI financial transition models estimate the one-time capital infrastructure cost of establishing a new provincial capital (civil secretariat complex, provincial assembly building, High Court benches, and ministerial housing) between PKR 35 billion and PKR 65 billion (in FY2026 prices). However, recurrent annual administrative operating expenditures represent less than 3.5% of the new province's projected NFC divisible pool allocation, making territorial reorganization financially sustainable if phased over 3 years.

11. How many administrative divisions and districts are in Pakistan?

As of 2026, Pakistan is divided into 38 administrative divisions and approximately 164 gazetted and listed districts across the 4 provinces and federal territories (Punjab: 11 divisions, 41 districts; Sindh: 7 divisions, 30 districts; Khyber Pakhtunkhwa: 7 divisions, 37 districts; Balochistan: 8 divisions, 37 districts; ICT: 1 district; Gilgit-Baltistan: 3 divisions, 14 districts; Azad Jammu & Kashmir: 3 divisions, 10 districts).

12. What is the difference between a province and a division?

A province is a sovereign constitutional constituent unit of the federation with its own elected legislature (Provincial Assembly), Chief Minister, cabinet, independent High Court, and constitutional taxation and NFC rights under the Constitution of Pakistan. A division is purely an administrative echelon within a province headed by a senior civil servant (Divisional Commissioner) who coordinates revenue, police, and development functions across multiple districts without legislative or constitutional status.

13. How does the Provincial Finance Commission (PFC) award work in Pakistan?

The Provincial Finance Commission (PFC) award is the subnational mechanism through which provincial governments distribute a designated share of their total revenues (from provincial taxes and federal NFC transfers) to local governments (District Councils, Municipal Corporations, and Town Councils). While mandated under provincial local government legislation, regular formula-based PFC awards have remained largely in abeyance in Punjab and Sindh since 2008, causing fiscal centralism in provincial capitals.

14. Can Gilgit-Baltistan become a fifth province of Pakistan?

Granting Gilgit-Baltistan full constitutional provincial status requires amending Article 1 and Article 59 of the Constitution of Pakistan to give GB representation in the National Assembly and Senate. However, this move has historically been constrained by Pakistan's international position on the United Nations Security Council resolutions concerning the broader Jammu & Kashmir dispute. Proposed solutions include granting GB provisional provincial status pending final international settlement.

15. What is the 18th Constitutional Amendment and how did it affect devolution?

Passed unanimously in April 2010, the 18th Amendment abolished the Federal Concurrent Legislative List, devolving 17 federal ministries (including health, education, local government, agriculture, and environment) to the provinces. It also strengthened provincial fiscal autonomy through Article 160(3A)—which prohibits reducing the provincial NFC share below 57.5%—and established 50-50 co-ownership over natural resources under Article 172(3). However, critics note it stopped devolution at provincial capitals rather than devolving powers downwards to local governments.

16. What is the average household income in Pakistan according to HIES 2024–25?

According to the Household Integrated Economic Survey (HIES 2024–25) released by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, the average nominal monthly household income in Pakistan is PKR 82,179. Mean monthly income is PKR 96,767 for urban households and PKR 72,157 for rural households. These are household averages, not individual wages or median income. HIES 2024–25 reports an average household size of 5.98 nationally (5.69 in urban areas and 6.18 in rural areas).

17. Which is the largest province of Pakistan by area and by population?

Balochistan is the largest province of Pakistan by land area, covering 347,190 square kilometers (approximately 43.6% of Pakistan's total territory). Punjab is by far the largest province by population, housing 127,688,922 citizens according to the 2023 Census (52.88% of the national total).

18. What was the 2001 Devolution Plan under the National Reconstruction Bureau (NRB)?

The Local Government Ordinance (LGO) 2001 introduced a revolutionary 3-tier devolution architecture: District Governments (headed by an elected Zila Nazim), Tehsil Municipal Administrations (headed by a Tehsil Nazim), and Union Administrations (headed by a Union Nazim). Crucially, the District Coordination Officer (DCO) and district line department heads were made administratively subordinate to the elected Zila Nazim, subordinating the colonial civil bureaucracy to elected representatives.

19. What is the PPRI-01 13-province model?

PPRI-01 is an illustrative, first-pass cartographic and administrative reorganization model that divides Pakistan into 13 balanced provincial units using historical divisional clusters: 4 in Punjab (West Punjab, South Punjab, Bahawalpur, Potohar), 3 in Sindh (Urban Sindh/Karachi, Rural Sindh, Mehran), 4 in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (Khyber, South KPK, Hazara, Kohistan), and 2 in Balochistan (Balochistan, Makran), alongside federal overlays for Karachi and Gwadar.

20. How does administrative distance to provincial secretariats affect service delivery in Pakistan?

Administrative distance measures the physical travel time and bureaucratic friction required for a citizen or district officer to reach the provincial seat of power. In expansive provinces like Balochistan (Gwadar is over 900 km from Quetta) or South Punjab (Rajanpur is over 550 km from Lahore), excessive distance creates severe delays in project approvals, teacher transfers, emergency medical supplies, and legal appeals at High Courts.

21. What are the powers of Tehsil and District Councils in Pakistan?

Tehsil Councils are responsible for local municipal infrastructure, town planning, building control, water supply schemes, solid waste disposal, street lighting, and local road maintenance. District Councils traditionally coordinate inter-tehsil infrastructure, district roads, rural development programs, and sub-provincial development coordination with line departments.

22. How are seats allocated in the National Assembly and Senate for new provinces?

National Assembly seats are allocated strictly according to population under Article 51 of the Constitution (using official Census figures); thus, creating new provinces does not change a region's total National Assembly seats. In the Senate, however, Article 59 guarantees equal representation (23 senators per province regardless of population). Redrawing provincial limits therefore requires amending Article 59 to maintain legislative balance in the federal upper house.
