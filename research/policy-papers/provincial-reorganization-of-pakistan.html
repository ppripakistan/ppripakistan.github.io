The Provincial Reorganization of Pakistan

Constitutional, administrative, fiscal, demographic and local-government considerations for an evidence-based national debate on territorial governance.

Executive Summary

Pakistan's debate over provincial reorganization is ultimately a question of how political authority, public administration, fiscal resources and representation should be organized. The Constitution establishes a federal structure, four provinces in Article 1, a local-government obligation under Article 140A, an intergovernmental fiscal framework under Article 160, and a demanding constitutional-amendment procedure under Article 239.

A serious proposal must consider geography, administrative access, institutional capacity, fiscal sustainability, representation, transition costs, local government and the constitutional process. Additional provinces are one possible institutional pathway among several; stronger local government and other decentralization arrangements can address some of the same objectives through different mechanisms.

1. The problem

Large and diverse administrative units can create questions about political representation, access to provincial institutions, distribution of development resources and the distance between citizens and decision-makers. Creating additional provinces can also introduce new administrative structures, fiscal obligations, political institutions and transition costs. Both sides of the problem require evidence.

Under Pakistan's current configuration, a citizen in Rajanpur or Fort Munro travels over 600 kilometers to reach the provincial civil secretariat in Lahore. Similarly, a resident of Gwadar must travel upwards of 14 hours across rugged terrain to reach Quetta. Such severe spatial frictions distort state responsiveness and breed legitimate feelings of peripheral exclusion.

2. Constitutional baseline

Article 1 identifies Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh as provinces. Article 140A requires provincial legislation establishing local government and devolving political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority to elected local representatives. Article 160 establishes the National Finance Commission framework. Article 239 sets out constitutional amendment procedures and requires two-thirds approval of the affected Provincial Assembly where provincial limits would be altered.

Constitutional Flowchart: Article 239(4) Boundary Amendment Pathway

01
Bill Introduction: Constitutional Amendment Bill introduced in either the National Assembly or the Senate (Article 239(1)).
02
Federal Parliament Supermajority: Passed by not less than two-thirds of total membership in both the National Assembly and the Senate (Article 239(2)).
03
Provincial Veto Shield: If the bill alters provincial limits, it cannot be presented to the President unless passed by the affected Provincial Assembly by at least two-thirds of its total membership (Article 239(4)).
04
Presidential Assent & Gazette: Submission to the President for mandatory assent, followed by official statutory gazette notification and institutional demarcation.

4. What arguments can support additional provinces?

Supporters argue that smaller provincial units could bring provincial institutions closer to residents, create additional political representation, reduce administrative distance and allow governance systems to be designed around more geographically coherent territories. Specifically, advocates highlight:

  • Span of Control: De-congesting single secretariats managing upwards of 125+ million citizens.
  • Equal Senate Voice: Creating new provincial entities would grant historically marginalized peripheral zones equal 23-seat blocks in the upper house of parliament.
  • Targeted Economic Planning: Formulating regional annual development plans tailored to distinct ecological and agrarian zones rather than monolithic megacity priorities.

5. What reservations require testing?

Reservations include the cost of establishing additional governments, duplication of departments, division of assets and liabilities, uncertainty over revenue and transfers, disputes over boundaries and capitals, personnel allocation and the possibility that a new provincial tier does not solve problems originating at the local-government level.

PPRI Six-Pillar Reorganization Assessment Matrix

PPRI evaluates any territorial restructuring proposal against six non-negotiable structural criteria:

1. Demographic Balance Prevents single-unit hegemony without creating demographically unviable micro-units.
2. Fiscal Viability Must possess a credible path to generate own-source tax revenue beyond federal transfers.
3. Spatial Proximity Ensures no citizen is situated greater than 3.5 hours from a High Court bench or Secretariat.
4. Social Cohesion Prevents zero-sum ethnic fragmentation by respecting pluralism and historical shared geography.
5. Third-Tier Subsidiarity Mandates statutory local government protection so new provincial elites cannot hijack municipal funds.
6. Federation Safeguards Maintains Senate equality and intergovernmental dispute mechanisms under the Council of Common Interests (CCI).

6. Provinces and local government

Article 140A is central because it provides a constitutional basis for elected local government and devolution within provinces. A territorial reform proposal that leaves local authority weak may not address all access and accountability problems that motivate reform arguments. Conversely, stronger local government could address some governance objectives without creating another provincial administration.

7. Fiscal architecture

Article 160 establishes the National Finance Commission mechanism for distribution of specified revenues between the Federation and Provinces and for grants, borrowing powers and related fiscal matters. A new province would need a sustainable fiscal arrangement for recurring expenditure as well as a transition plan for one-time costs. Population, revenue capacity, expenditure needs, transfers and equalization should be modeled separately.

Under the current 7th NFC Award framework, 82% of the divisible pool is allocated on population alone. Dividing a province mechanically divides its population share, leaving the aggregate fiscal pie unaltered while substantially increasing recurrent administrative overheads.

A province requires more than a name and boundary. A transition plan must address civil cadres, court systems, secretariats, and physical infrastructure:

9. Research models

Model A — Existing structure + stronger local government

Test whether deeper local-government devolution addresses defined governance problems without redrawing provincial boundaries.

Model B — Additional provinces

Test territorial restructuring against measurable representation, administrative and fiscal objectives under Article 239.

Model C — Provincial + local reform

Combine territorial restructuring with a defined constitutional local-government settlement to ensure dual subsidiarity.

Model D — Other decentralization

Examine administrative, fiscal or metropolitan arrangements (such as autonomous divisional secretariats) that do not require constitutional amendments.

10. Evidence framework

Future PPRI scenario studies will publish underlying indicators: population served, geographic area, access measures where available, institutional setup costs, recurring fiscal balance, own-source revenue, transfers, service coverage, representation, administrative capacity and legal requirements.

11. Constitutional process

Where a proposal alters provincial limits, Article 239 is especially important. A constitutional amendment bill must pass each House under the specified two-thirds requirement, and where provincial limits are altered it also requires passage by the affected Provincial Assembly by not less than two-thirds of its total membership before presidential assent.

12. Conclusion

Pakistan's provincial structure is a legitimate subject for serious public-policy research. The evidence supports a broad research agenda rather than a one-dimensional answer: measure administrative distance, representation, fiscal sustainability, institutional capacity and service delivery; compare territorial reform with local-government and other decentralization options; and publish the constitutional and financial implications transparently.
