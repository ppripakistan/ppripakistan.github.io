The Price of a Simple Task

How much does Pakistan's bureaucracy cost citizens?

Executive summary

A government service has an official price.

There may be a fee for a certificate, a license, a permit or a document. That is the number most governments publish.

But that is not necessarily what the citizen pays.

The citizen may also pay for:

  • a bus ride to an office;
  • a day away from work;
  • a photocopy;
  • a second trip because one document was missing;
  • a queue;
  • a middleman;
  • an unofficial payment;
  • a visit to another office;
  • another form;
  • another signature;
  • another month waiting for a file to move.

Pakistan already has several large government systems whose existence is a signal that administrative friction is not a marginal problem. The Pakistan Citizen's Portal reports more than 6.6 million complaints since inception and over 6.5 million marked resolved. The Wafaqi Mohtasib received 226,372 complaints in 2024 and recorded a very high institutional redressal rate. Punjab's e-Khidmat programme was explicitly created around ideas such as simplifying procedures, improving accessibility, reducing citizen cost and reducing opportunities for corruption.

Yet Pakistan does not have a single public measure answering a deceptively simple question:

How much time and money does an ordinary person lose to complete an ordinary government task?

That gap is itself a research problem.

This paper proposes that Pakistan should begin measuring the Citizen Cost of the State: not only the official cost of a service, but the total time, travel, documentation, waiting, repeat-visit and informal-cost burden associated with obtaining it.

The paper uses existing complaint, ombudsman and citizen-facilitation evidence to show why the issue matters, then proposes a PPRI Administrative Burden Index that can be built from direct process audits and a representative citizen survey.

The argument is straightforward:

When government is inefficient, the bill does not disappear. It is transferred from the government to the citizen.

1. A government fee is only the beginning

Imagine that a government certificate officially costs Rs500.

That sounds cheap.

Now imagine that obtaining it requires:

  • one trip to an office;
  • a four-hour queue;
  • two photocopies;
  • another trip because the application was missing a stamp;
  • transport of Rs700 each way;
  • one day of missed work;
  • an agent who offers to “handle the file” for Rs2,000.

The official price is still Rs500.

The real price is not.

This distinction is rarely visible in public budgets.

When governments discuss the cost of delivering a service, they usually calculate:

staff + buildings + computers + electricity + administrative expense.

The citizen experiences a different calculation:

fee + travel + time + uncertainty + paperwork + lost wages + informal cost.

A functioning state minimizes both.

A weak administrative system often counts only the first.

2. Pakistan's own reforms reveal the problem

Punjab's e-Khidmat system provides an unusually candid example of how governments themselves describe administrative difficulty.

The programme presents one-window service as a way to provide:

  • easier access;
  • prompt service;
  • simplified procedures;
  • greater transparency;
  • reduced opportunities for corruption;
  • lower cost for citizens and government.

That is important.

The government did not create a one-window system because citizens were already receiving services effortlessly.

It created it because the older system involved enough friction to justify redesign.

This is a general principle:

When a government launches a programme to reduce administrative burden, the existence of the programme is evidence that administrative burden was considered a real problem.

The next question is whether we measure how much burden remains.

3. The complaints mountain

The Pakistan Citizen's Portal provides another window into the demand for government correction.

At its current public dashboard snapshot, the Portal reports:

  • approximately 4.76 million registered citizens;
  • more than 6.66 million complaints;
  • more than 6.55 million complaints marked resolved.

The public dashboard's 'resolved' number is a portal status. It is not the same as independently verified resolution or citizen satisfaction.

Those figures are cumulative platform totals, not a national census of all grievances. A complaint marked “resolved” is also an administrative status; it should not automatically be interpreted as proof that the citizen was satisfied or that the underlying problem disappeared.

Nevertheless, the scale matters.

Millions of people have used a national system to tell the state:

Something did not work.

The portal therefore acts like a giant signpost.

It says there is enough friction in public services for citizens to spend time opening complaints, waiting for responses and following up.

The research opportunity is to go one level deeper.

What are these complaints about?

How many are generated because of:

  • delays?
  • missing information?
  • unclear procedures?
  • non-responsive officials?
  • service failure?
  • land disputes?
  • utilities?
  • police?
  • municipal problems?
  • social-protection failures?

And how many could have been prevented if the original service had been designed better?

4. The ombudsman tells the same story from another angle

The Wafaqi Mohtasib is not a general complaint app. It is an institution designed to address maladministration.

In 2024 it reported 226,372 complaints received and about 223,198 complaints redressed, along with an implementation rate of about 93.21% for decisions.

The high redressal volume should not be interpreted as evidence that the federal government is malfunctioning in every one of those cases. The Mohtasib receives complaints from people with very different problems, and many are resolved after intervention.

But an ombudsman exists for a reason.

Fact Check — Wafaqi Mohtasib: Mohtasib redressal/implementation statistics are institutional reporting. They demonstrate workload and institutional outcomes but are not a national measure of all maladministration.

A citizen should normally be able to obtain a public service without needing an independent grievance institution to force an agency to respond.

The complaint mechanism is therefore a second-order cost.

The person first spends time obtaining the service.

Then, when it fails, the person spends more time complaining about the failure.

That is duplicated effort.

5. The citizen pays twice

Consider a hypothetical case.

A worker needs a certificate for a job application.

First cost

Travel to the government office.

Second cost

Time waiting.

Third cost

Lost wages.

Fourth cost

Return journey.

Fifth cost

A second visit.

Sixth cost

A complaint.

The government may describe this as:

“Service delivered.”

The worker experiences it as:

“I had to spend an entire day getting a piece of paper.”

This difference between administrative reporting and lived experience is one of the most important subjects PPRI can study.

6. Administrative burden is a productivity issue

It is tempting to treat bureaucracy as merely an annoyance.

It is not.

Suppose a city has 1 million working-age people.

If even 10% of them spend one unnecessary working day each year dealing with avoidable administrative procedures, that is:

100,000 working days.

If each lost working day is worth Rs2,000 in wages or productive output, the opportunity cost would be:

Rs200 million.

That is an illustration, not an estimate of Pakistan's actual cost.

But it demonstrates the logic.

Administrative inefficiency acts like a hidden tax on productive time.

For a salaried worker, the cost may be a missed shift.

For a shopkeeper, a closed shop.

For a farmer, a missed market day.

For a student, a missed class.

For a small business, delayed registration.

For a woman who must depend on another family member for travel, the cost may be even greater because the burden is not simply monetary.

7. Delay is not distributed equally

The same government process can be cheap for one person and extremely expensive for another.

Consider two applicants.

Applicant A

Lives near the district headquarters, has a flexible job, internet access and private transport.

Applicant B

Lives 80 kilometres away, works on a daily wage, depends on public transport and cannot easily afford another trip.

The application fee may be identical.

The government sees equality.

The citizens experience inequality.

This is why administrative burden is a regional and social-policy issue.

A bureaucratic hurdle becomes more expensive as distance, poverty and insecure employment increase.

8. A queue is not just a queue

The most underappreciated cost of public administration may be waiting.

A person waiting four hours in a government office is not doing nothing in an economic sense.

They may be:

  • losing wages;
  • paying for transport;
  • missing school or university;
  • delaying a business transaction;
  • delaying treatment;
  • paying somebody else to perform work they would normally do.

Waiting time is therefore an economic input.

It is also a measure of state capacity.

A well-designed service should aim to reduce:

uncertainty + unnecessary visits + unnecessary waiting.

9. The paperwork problem

A process can be slow without corruption.

It can be corrupt without being slow.

It can also be both.

The important point is that administrative complexity creates opportunities.

Every additional:

  • signature;
  • form;
  • document;
  • office;
  • approval;
  • discretionary decision

creates another point at which the citizen can become stuck.

It also creates another point at which an intermediary can offer to “make things easier.”

This is why anti-corruption policy and process simplification are connected.

A complicated process can create a market for middlemen.

A transparent, predictable, digital process can shrink that market.

10. The e-Khidmat experiment is important

Punjab's e-Khidmat model is useful because it attacks several parts of the problem at once.

Instead of:

office A → office B → office C

it aims for:

one place → multiple services.

This changes the citizen's transaction.

It reduces the number of places the person must navigate.

That matters even where the official fee is unchanged.

The lesson is not:

One-window centres solve bureaucracy.

The evidence is not sufficient to make that sweeping claim.

The more defensible lesson is:

Administrative design can change the citizen's cost even without changing the underlying government benefit.

That is exactly what PPRI should measure.

11. The missing national number

Pakistan has information on:

  • complaints;
  • processing standards;
  • public facilitation;
  • individual government services;
  • ombudsman cases;
  • e-government initiatives.
Fact Check — Administrative burden: Pakistan does not currently have a definitive national public number for total hours lost by citizens to bureaucracy. This paper proposes PPRI's measurement framework rather than inventing a national total.

But these sources do not currently produce a single, consistent national measure such as:

Average hours spent obtaining a public service.

or:

Average number of visits required.

or:

Average travel cost.

or:

Average informal payment.

That is the gap.

PPRI can fill it.

12. A PPRI Administrative Burden Index

We propose a new measure:

PPRI Administrative Burden Index (PABI)

The index would initially measure five dimensions.

1. Time

  • stated processing time;
  • actual processing time;
  • waiting time;
  • time spent following up.

2. Visits

  • number of physical visits;
  • number of offices;
  • repeat visits.

3. Documentation

  • documents officially required;
  • duplicate documents;
  • documents requested beyond the published list.

4. Monetary burden

  • official fee;
  • transport;
  • photocopies/printing;
  • intermediary/agent cost;
  • lost income.

5. Uncertainty

  • ability to track the application;
  • clarity of requirements;
  • likelihood of rejection for reasons unclear to the applicant;
  • availability of complaint/escalation.

We would publish each component separately before ever publishing a single composite number.

That matters because composite indexes can hide what is actually wrong.

13. How we should measure it

The first PPRI study should not try to measure every government service.

Start with perhaps 20 high-frequency services.

For example:

  • birth certificate;
  • death certificate;
  • domicile;
  • police character certificate;
  • vehicle registration;
  • driving licence;
  • land record/document;
  • marriage registration;
  • business registration;
  • building approval;
  • utility complaint;
  • social-protection enrolment;
  • school-related certificates;
  • health-related certificates;
  • identity-related services.

For each service, collect:

official process map

official fee

official processing time

number of steps

number of visits

digital availability

required documents

Then survey actual users.

14. The citizen survey

PPRI should ask questions that government systems often don't.

How many times did you visit?

How long did the whole process take?

How much did travel cost?

Did you lose income?

Did you need an intermediary?

Did anyone ask for money beyond the official fee?

Did you understand why the application was delayed?

Could you track it?

Did you complain?

Did the complaint solve the problem?

Would you use the same service again online if it existed?

These questions allow us to measure the difference between:

service available

and

service accessible.

15. Administrative accessibility should be a design principle

Consider two government websites.

Website A

Apply online.

But the person must still print a form, visit an office, submit physical documents and collect the final certificate.

Website B

Apply online → verify identity digitally → pay online → track status → receive certificate.

Technologically, both provide “online service.”

Functionally, they are completely different.

This is why digital-government statistics alone are insufficient.

We need to measure:

How much of the transaction has actually been digitized?

16. Digital government can also create new exclusion

Pakistan's household internet access rose dramatically, reaching around 70% nationally in HIES 2024–25.

But the urban-rural gap remains large:

82% urban

versus

61% rural.

Computer/laptop/tablet access is much lower than mobile/internet access.

That means replacing physical government offices with digital-only systems could reduce costs for some citizens while increasing barriers for others.

The goal should therefore be:

Digital where it removes friction; human support where digital access is weak.

The best government service is not the one with the most technology.

It is the one that requires the least unnecessary effort.

17. Corruption enters when predictability disappears

An unofficial payment often becomes attractive when the citizen believes:

“If I wait normally, I don't know when this will finish.”

That does not prove every delay is caused by corruption.

It does explain why uncertainty can create opportunities for intermediaries.

A transparent queue with:

  • fixed requirements;
  • published fees;
  • visible status;
  • binding deadlines;
  • traceable decisions

is harder to manipulate.

Administrative simplification is therefore not just an efficiency reform.

It can also be a corruption-control reform.

18. A useful analogy: the toll road

Imagine travelling from City A to City B.

One toll booth is annoying.

Ten unexplained toll booths are a system problem.

Now imagine that at every booth:

the fee is unclear;

the queue is unpredictable;

somebody outside the booth offers to “get you through faster.”

The journey has acquired an informal economy.

Government bureaucracy can work the same way.

Every unnecessary procedural stop creates another opportunity for friction.

Reduce the stops.

Publish the rules.

Digitize tracking.

Make deadlines enforceable.

The informal market for shortcuts becomes less attractive.

19. Administrative reform is also economic reform

The conventional reform discussion often counts savings to government.

For example:

fewer offices;

less paper;

fewer staff visits.

But the larger gain may occur outside government.

If millions of citizens spend fewer hours navigating public institutions, then those hours return to:

work
business
education
care
family
rest
productive activity.

That is why PPRI should eventually estimate the Citizen Time Dividend generated by successful administrative reform.

20. The regional dimension

The problem becomes even more important when the country is administratively large.

For a citizen in a remote district, decentralization isn't an abstract constitutional idea.

It can mean:

How far is the office?

Does the office exist locally?

Who can decide?

Can I appeal locally?

Do I have to travel to the provincial capital?

The World Bank's 2026 fiscal-federalism analysis repeatedly emphasizes the relationship between resource allocation, responsibilities and service delivery and argues that local-government financing needs to become more predictable.

This connects administrative burden directly to the PPRI provincial-reform question.

21. What success would look like

A good reform should be measurable.

Instead of:

“e-governance improved.”

PPRI should be able to say:

Average visits fell from 4.2 to 1.3.

Median waiting time fell from 5.5 hours to 45 minutes.

Average travel expenditure fell from Rs1,800 to Rs600.

Applications trackable online rose from 12% to 85%.

Those figures are examples of the type of measurement we need, not claims about current Pakistan.

That is the point of creating the index.

22. Three kinds of government efficiency

We propose separating:

Administrative efficiency

How efficiently does government process the file?

Citizen efficiency

How much effort must the person spend?

Social efficiency

What happens to the person's time, income and opportunity as a result?

A service can score well on the first and badly on the second.

The government officer may process 50 files a day.

The citizen may still have to make three visits.

That is not a contradiction.

It is a sign that the process is optimized around the bureaucracy rather than the user.

23. Why the local level matters

A central government or provincial government can issue a national rule.

But actual interaction with the state happens in places:

union council
municipal office
tehsil
district
land office
police station
hospital
school
service centre

That is why local government and administrative reform cannot be separated.

The closer the institution is to the citizen, the greater the potential to reduce distance and repetition — provided the institution has clear authority, money, competent staff and accountability.

Devolution without those things simply transfers responsibility without capability.

24. What existing evidence tells us — and what it doesn't

The existing evidence strongly supports the proposition that Pakistan has substantial demand for grievance and service-correction mechanisms.

It does not yet give us a clean national estimate of:

“Pakistanis lose X hours every year because of bureaucracy.”

We should not invent one.

That number is exactly the thing PPRI should go and measure.

That is the difference between an advocacy paper and original research.

25. The PPRI research programme

The immediate project should have three parts.

Phase 1 — Desk study

Catalogue 20 public services.

Build official process maps.

Record official fees, steps and deadlines.

Phase 2 — Mystery-user audit

Researchers independently attempt each process.

Record:

  • time;
  • visits;
  • documents;
  • waiting;
  • costs;
  • digital usability;
  • deviations from published procedure.

This should be done ethically and without provoking or entraping officials.

Phase 3 — Citizen survey

Survey users across:

  • major cities;
  • secondary cities;
  • rural districts;
  • different income groups;
  • women and men;
  • different provinces.

Then publish:

PABI score
service-by-service burden
regional difference
citizen experience

Conclusion: the cheapest government service may be the one that requires nothing from you

Government is often described through its budget.

But citizens experience government through transactions.

A birth certificate.

A land record.

A licence.

A permit.

A complaint.

A registration.

A benefit.

A visit.

A queue.

A signature.

A return trip.

The official fee can be small while the real cost is enormous.

That cost is paid in time, wages, transport, uncertainty and sometimes informal payments.

Pakistan's existing complaint and facilitation systems show that these frictions are real enough to require major government programmes. But the country lacks a coherent national measurement of the citizen burden created by the state itself.

PPRI should build one.

Because the ultimate test of administrative reform is not:

How many departments did government create?

It is:

How much effort does an ordinary person have to spend to get government to do something it is already supposed to do?

That is a number worth knowing.

And unlike another general survey of “bureaucratic inefficiency,” it is a number PPRI can actually begin measuring.
